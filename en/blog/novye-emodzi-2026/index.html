New Emoji 2026 — What's Coming in Emoji 18.0

Every year the Unicode Consortium approves a new batch of emoji, and each time it's a small event: billions of messages a day, and a fresh glyph spreads across messengers within weeks. In 2026 the Emoji 18.0 set arrives — but, unlike previous years, it's noticeably more compact. And that's the first thing that catches the eye and needs explaining: there are few new emoji not because ideas ran out, but because the flow itself changed its nature.

What's in Emoji 18.0

Let's start with what's actually being added. The Unicode working group proposed about nine new concepts for release in September 2026. Among the candidates — a squinting face the expression set was missing, two new thumb gestures expanding the "hand language," a pickle to fill out the food category, and a lighthouse for the "travel and places" category.

The exact final list and appearance depend on vendors, but even from this set the direction is visible: the emphasis shifted to facial expressions and everyday objects rather than exotica. And that's not an accident but a direct consequence of why there are fewer new glyphs.

Why there are fewer new emoji

A few years ago each year brought dozens, even a hundred, new glyphs. Now the flow has slowed sharply — and the numbers show it clearly:

It's easy to read this as a decline, but behind the numbers is not a crisis but the standard's maturing, and it has three reasons, each following from the last. First: the set is already big — the total number of emoji is approaching 4,000, and basic concepts have long been covered, so there are almost no "obvious" gaps left to urgently fill. Second: since the simple is covered, Unicode became stricter about selecting candidates so as not to breed rarely used glyphs — the bet shifted from quantity to quality. Third: much is now covered not by new symbols but by customization of existing ones — skin‑tone, direction, and gender variations — so one new emoji corresponds to many already‑available variants. That is, fewer glyphs are added, but the user's expressive options don't shrink.

This shift to customization is worth seeing in numbers, because it's exactly what explains the apparent paradox of "few newcomers in a rich set" A single "person" glyph with skin‑tone, gender, and sometimes gaze‑direction modifiers unfolds into dozens of ready combinations, and each of them is a separate, "own" emoji to the user. That is, where several different figures would once have been added, now one base one is added and the rest is covered by variations. Formally this is "few new emoji," but in practice it's a rise in the number of available variants. So judging a set's richness by the number of newcomers in a release is like measuring a dictionary by the number of words added in a year: the main body has long been in place, and the annual increment merely polishes the edges.

How an emoji reaches the keyboard

Understanding why even these nine glyphs won't appear on a phone right away is easiest by tracing an emoji's whole path — it's multi‑stage. It all starts with a proposal: anyone can submit an application for a new emoji with justification — frequency, need, no duplicate (how it's done, in how to propose your own emoji). Then comes Unicode selection: the working group evaluates candidates and approves a shortlist — that's the "draft" Then — standardization: the glyph gets a codepoint in the Unicode standard. After that comes vendor design: Apple, Google, Samsung, and others draw their own versions, which is why the same emoji looks different on different devices (why — in why emoji display differently). And only then does the glyph release in an OS update — reaching the user with a system update, sometimes a year after approval.

Why a new emoji "doesn't display"

It's precisely because of this delay between Unicode approval and a glyph appearing on devices that the familiar effect arises: until your OS updates, a new emoji shows as an empty box or a cross. This isn't an error — the system just doesn't know the symbol yet, and the standard and its picture reach the device at different times. A detailed breakdown — in why emoji don't display.

Emoji and brands in 2026

The slowdown of newcomers didn't make emoji less important — on the contrary, by 2026 they've finally left private chats and become a brand tool. In marketing, emoji in headings, posts, and newsletters boost visibility (how to use them wisely — in emoji in marketing). In business correspondence their appropriateness depends on context, and we cover the boundaries in emoji in business correspondence. And in chats themselves emoji increasingly compete for attention with stickers, GIFs, and avatars — yet remain the most universal "language" understood almost everywhere, whereas a sticker works only where it's recognized. The short takeaway

In 2026 the compact Emoji 18.0 set arrives — about nine new concepts, including a squinting face, gestures, a pickle, and a lighthouse. The small number of newcomers isn't a decline but the standard's maturing: basic concepts are already covered, so Unicode bets on selection quality and on customization of existing glyphs rather than on the quantity of new ones. And even an approved glyph won't appear on a phone right away: each vendor draws its own version, and newcomers arrive only with an OS update — so a fresh emoji may show as an empty box for a while, and that's normal.
