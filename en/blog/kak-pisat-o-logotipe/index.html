How to Write About a Logo: Why Strong Marks Fail on Words

In 2008 the Arnell Group delivered Pepsi a 27-page document titled "Breathtaking." Inside: the golden ratio, Pythagoras, the Mona Lisa, the Parthenon, and the "gravitational pull of Pepsi," from which the new shape of the mark was derived. The contract cost around a million dollars. The logo itself moved by a hair: the circle was rounded slightly, the white wave tilted.

A year later the document leaked and became the thing everyone discussed. The redesign faded into the background while "Breathtaking" turned into shorthand, cited whenever an explanation sounds louder than the work. The mark survived and served until its replacement in 2023; the reputational damage went to the text.

The case is extreme, the mechanics are ordinary. A logo is shown in silence for ten seconds and explained for twenty minutes, and those twenty minutes decide its fate. So the text about a mark deserves the same requirements the mark itself gets: one readable idea, verifiable statements in place of adjectives, and nothing that can be cut without loss.

Why the client judges the mark by your explanation

The objection is immediate: strong work should speak for itself. In a presentation it doesn't, for a mechanical reason. The designer sees a grid, optical compensations, and how the shape behaves shrunk to an icon; the client sees a picture and compares it with the one they imagined on the way over. Only speech closes that distance.

The distance grows as the client's knowledge shrinks. A first‑time buyer of a logo hears "monogram" as jargon and "it fails at favicon size" as nitpicking. Someone who survived a rebrand expects terms, deadlines, and limits of use. One text won't convince both: the first needs an easy entrance, the second specifics.

With no explanation, the distance fills itself predictably. A client with nothing to say about the mark's job talks about details instead: "play with the typeface," nudge that element, show it in blue. Such requests mean one thing — the person got no criterion for accepting the mark and reached for the only instrument left, their taste. An endless run of revisions follows, because taste has no stopping condition.

Hence the step before the deck opens: decide what the person should understand, feel, and do once you finish. And do — one thing only. "Approve the primary mark" and "choose among three directions" need different texts; chasing both leaves the client thinking it over, and the meeting ends with "let us look again, show it on a business card." That action is easier to define when the opening brief fixed the mark's job and the budget and stages settled how many rounds of edits are included.

The target action tells you what to talk about. What holds those words together is something else — the single idea underneath them.

A thesis works in text the way one idea works in a mark

A logo carrying two ideas at once reads as neither: the eye hunts for the main one, finds two candidates, and leaves. For the same reason brands spent decades stripping details from their marks — every extra meaning steals attention from the primary one. Text behaves identically: until it has one sentence everything is built around, the listener collects facts and assembles a picture alone, usually the wrong one.

Here is such a sentence in practice. Phil Knight's 1971 brief came down to one task: a mark that conveys motion and doesn't get confused with Adidas's three stripes. Carolyn Davidson logged 17.5 hours and invoiced 35 dollars; Knight looked at the swoosh and said, "I don't love it, but maybe it'll grow on me." Not a gram of enthusiasm, and the idea that landed was the intended one — what the Nike mark has stood on for half a century.

Each of these marks carries a single idea, which is why the picture alone suffices:

Such a sentence is easier to find backwards, starting from the job the mark is placed into instead of the shape already drawn. Look at where the logo will work most often and decide what must happen to a person there within half a second. A coffee chain needs recognition from across the street; a banking app, distinction among thirty icons on a home screen; a marketplace, visibility in a feed packed with other people's cards. Three answers set three requirements for the shape and three defences, none of which sounds like "modern and memorable." The check is simple: if your sentence sits unedited under a stranger's mark from an adjacent category, work on the thesis hasn't started.

The retelling test checks your finished text for the same wholeness. The client will describe the mark to colleagues in one sentence; the question is which. "Something modern with smooth lines" means there was no idea. "A mark about motion," "a mark readable from thirty metres on a sign," "a mark that assembles from one module for every service" mean it landed. The test catches the opposite extreme too: assign a mark five hidden meanings and the audience remembers none.

One thesis carries the weight of a text only when supports stand under it. The weakest support is built from adjectives.

"Modern and dynamic" proves nothing

Those words describe the speaker's state and never touch the mark. Any shape can be called modern, any tilt dynamic, any black premium. The client hears them at every other presentation, tunes them out, and falls back on the only criterion available — like it or not. That is how strong concepts lose to weak ones, and the mistake belongs to the defence: by then the artwork had done everything it could.

An adjective can almost always be swapped for a claim the client can verify in a minute.

What gets saidWhat to show instead
"Unique"No mark with a similar shape in the trademark registry for the relevant class — an evening's work to check
"Scales well"The silhouette stays legible at a 16-pixel favicon instead of collapsing into a blob
"Universal"The mark works in a single colour — for engraving, stamps, and fax
"Visible"Contrast against the background from 3:1, the WCAG threshold for non‑text elements, so the mark holds up in dark mode
"Flexible"Three shape variants drawn for three size ranges — an adaptive system

Numbers work where adjectives fail. "The logo cost Nike 35 dollars" still gets retold; "the logo cost Nike very little" would be forgotten. The same move rescues money and timing: "colour revisions take two days" sounds calmer than "revisions will be handled promptly," though the second promise is more generous.

Verifiable phrasing has a side effect worth preparing for: the client answers in the coordinate system you set. Told "legible at 16 pixels," they check the favicon mockup and say "it blurs together here" — a note you can act on within the hour. Told "modern and stylish," they answer "it just doesn't grab me," and there is nothing to act on. Criteria stated before the reveal turn an argument about taste into a task list and draw the revision boundary: work against a named criterion belongs to the stage, work against a mood gets discussed separately.

The facts are gathered and checkable. From here the result depends on the order you deliver them in.

Where to start a defence so it gets heard out

Arnell started with Pythagoras and reached the shape of the mark near the end of the document. Everyone's attention had run out by then: by page twenty the reader had already concluded no substantive point was coming and kept going for the jokes.

The reverse order works. First sentence — what the mark solves; after that — how it is built. "One file covers the sign, the app, and the paperwork" before "the construction rests on an eight‑unit modular grid." The client evaluates the first immediately because it speaks the language of their problems, and accepts the second as its justification, provided they are still listening. The rule extends to length: strong words go in a sentence's first two or three, because its end doesn't always get read.

The same order rescues talk about process. "We'll run a competitor study" describes your labour; "we'll show you the seven shapes already in your category so you don't end up with an eighth one just like them" describes the client's outcome and heads off an argument about the invoice. There's a separate breakdown of that whole conversation from first email to file handover, written from the client's side.

In a presentation a living person keeps the order: spot a wandering gaze, cut it short. A brand book holds dozens of times more text, gets read without you and out of sequence, and needs a stricter check.

What three tests reveal when you run them over a brand book

A brand book is rarely read front to back. People open the needed spread, take the rule, and close it. So the document quietly falls apart into sections living on their own, and six months later a contractor's designer produces a layout that breaks no stated rule and still looks foreign. Three questions to the draft check the connective tissue.

Swap. Exchange two adjacent sections. If the document barely changed, there was no link between them — just two pages on a shared topic. In a coherent brand book the clear‑space section follows the minimum‑size section precisely because the second explains the first.

Delete. Remove a section and read the next one. Reads fine — the removed section was sitting there on the strength of being interesting. A two‑page company origin story is usually exactly that: no rule for the mark follows from it, and the contractor pages past it.

Conjunction. Mentally insert a connective that matches the relation: "therefore," "in order to," "then," "but," "that is." If one fits naturally, the link is real. If the only candidates are "by the way," "also," and "furthermore," the chain has already torn and you're holding a pile of unrelated requirements.

A strong brand book passes all three because it is built as a chain: positioning sets the character of the mark, the character constrains the palette, the palette dictates the rules for dark backgrounds, and those rules produce the clear‑space requirements. That chain delivers brand consistency in other people's hands: the contractor sees the reason behind each restriction and stops hunting for workarounds. The brand voice section takes the same test — if no concrete rule for wording follows from the mission and values, the section is decorative.

The architecture passed the tests, and the text can still ring hollow. The cause is a set of phrasings that, in 2026, gives itself away in the first line.

Which turns of phrase expose a machine‑written description

As generators spread, brand descriptions grew more alike than the brands themselves. The tool has nothing to do with it — AI genuinely helps in branding at the stage of options and routine. What makes a text recognisable is the set of phrasings arriving with the draft, and clients already read them, the way they spot an AI-generated logo by its telltale symmetry and surplus detail.

The first marker is the "not X but Y" construction in all its forms: "this is not just a logo but a brand promise," "we created not a picture but a system," "not only a mark but a character." It shows up in generated text by the dozen and is cured by rewriting into the affirmative.

BeforeAfter
This is not just a logo but a systemThe logo sets the rules for every carrier
We developed not a picture but a recognition toolThe mark earns recognition from thirty metres
It is important to note that in today's world a brand must be flexibleThe mark assembles from one module for each service

Next come the filler connectives — "it is important to understand," "it is worth noting," "thus," "ultimately": each occupies space and adds no meaning. Then vague adjectives with no attribute behind them: "real," "proper," "truly unique." The last sign shows before you read a word, from one glance at the page: three consecutive paragraphs of identical length and construction. A live author breaks rhythm; a generator holds it steady.

Count your metaphors

"Brand DNA," "the soul of the company," "a visual anchor," "the foundation of communication" — individually each metaphor is tolerable; together they squeeze the last of the specifics out of a description, leaving nothing in the paragraph to verify. Keep no more than two across a whole document, and keep the one that simplifies understanding. If an image needs a sentence of its own to explain it, it is in the way.

The phrasings are cleaned up and the document holds together. That leaves the question of what it is all for — a presentation lasts an hour, and the text stays forever.

Where this text lives after the presentation

Approval ends the story for the designer alone. The same words then scatter into places where you are absent: the site description, an image alt attribute, the brand page's meta description, a rebrand announcement, a support reply to "why did your icon change." Each inherits the quality of the original text, because nobody rewrites a formulation from scratch.

The scale shows in any catalogue. Trace Logo's holds 746 logos, and 640 carry a brand history: a few paragraphs on where the shape came from and what changed across redesigns. A card with that text and a card without it differ at once — the second looks like a placeholder, though the picture is identical. A brand with a mark and no explanation gets the same effect.

The shortest formats break most often. An image's alt attribute is read by screen readers and search crawlers, and "company logo" tells neither anything — what works is "T-Bank logo: a white shield with a T on a yellow square." A meta description gets cut at roughly 160 characters, so an adjective up front eats the space a fact needed. A caption under the mark outlives the deck itself: people copy it into an email, a chat, an appendix to the contract. In all three the space runs out immediately, and whatever missed the first few words never reaches the reader.

One audience appeared recently. AI search and assistants answer questions like "what does the T-Bank logo look like" by retelling other people's pages, and what survives the retelling is whatever is stated verifiably: the year the mark changed, the author, what exactly moved. Adjectives never survive — there is nothing in them to quote. So optimising for AI search starts where concept defence ends: with facts that have a source.

Logo changes show it most sharply. When X replaced the blue bird with a Latin letter in 2023, the official explanation ran to a couple of lines and the market wrote the rest itself; versions outnumbered facts. When T-Bank changed its name in 2024, every step came with specifics: what changes in the app, what on the cards, on what schedule. The first left an argument behind, the second a clear retelling. The difference sits in the text, and it decides what your redesign or refresh becomes when the time comes to change the mark.

What to remember

A logo's fate is decided by the words that present it. "Breathtaking" shows it at the extreme: the Pepsi mark lived another fifteen years while the document got laughed at. So the text deserves the requirements the mark gets.

One idea the listener will retell in a single sentence. Verifiable claims instead of adjectives: "legible at 16 pixels" instead of "scales well," "no similar shape in the registry" instead of "unique." The conclusion in the first sentence, the construction in the second. Three coherence tests for anything longer than a page: swap, delete, conjunction. And the "not X but Y" phrasings cleaned out, after which the text stops sounding generated.

Practising on someone else's work is easier: open any card in the catalogue and retell the description of the mark in one sentence. Where that works, the text was done right.
