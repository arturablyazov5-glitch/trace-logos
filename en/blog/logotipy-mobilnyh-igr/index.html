Mobile Game Logos — Why They All Use Chunky 3D Letters

Open the games category of our catalog right after banks and you enter another world: chunky golden letters, outlines, characters, zero minimalism. Game logos defiantly break everything the "proper branding" articles teach — and do it on purpose. Here's why games have their own laws and what they can teach everyone else.

TL;DR

A game logo is product packaging on the app‑store shelf, not a corporate mark: it must sell a genre and an emotion within one second of scrolling. Hence the category canon: chunky outlined 3D letters (Brawl Stars, Clash of Clans), a character in the icon, "expensive" metal‑and‑gold textures. Minimalism doesn't work here — it doesn't sell fun.

The logo as packaging

The category's key: a player decides to install within seconds, scrolling past thousands of competitors. A game logo plays the role of product packaging — like a cereal box on a shelf: not "expressing brand values" but shouting "fun/epic/scary here" louder than the neighbors. Game marks are judged by poster laws, not the identity laws that shape app icons.

The Supercell canon: chunky outlined letters

The style that became the genre standard was honed by Supercell: Clash of Clans, Clash Royale, Brawl Stars, Hay Day — one formula throughout: meaty 3D letters with dark outlines, gold or colored bevels and a slight tilt. Why it works:

  • The outline separates the mark from any background — a battle screenshot, a banner, a video.
  • Volume and gold signal "a rich world, premium production" — texture carries meaning here.
  • The tilt and bouncing letters convey motion: a static sober typeface would read as office software — a clear case of how type sets character.

A character instead of a mark

The category's second pillar is mascots. The Angry Birds 2 icon houses the red bird; Among Us has its astronaut — a planet‑scale meme. A character does what letters can't: it can be loved. It lives in stickers, merch and memes, advertising the game for free — the same logic behind branded stickers on Telegram.

Genshin Impact: the Asian school's exception

Genshin Impact shows another tradition: elegant thin‑serif typography with no shouting volume. Asian RPGs sell not "fun bubblegum" but "a grand beautiful adventure" — their logos sit closer to film posters than arcades. A useful reminder: the category canon is not law but a reflection of genre. A casual game styled like Genshin would lose installs; an RPG styled like Brawl Stars would lose credibility.

Icon vs logo: the game mark's double life

A game's mark lives two parallel lives ruled by different laws. The logo (the name on the splash screen and store page) is a poster: chunky letters, textures, full composition. The icon is the shelf front in the store grid, where something else wins: a character's face shot large, maximum contrast, minimal text. Look at Brawl Stars or Clash Royale: the icon contains not a single letter of the name — only a close‑up character. Studios A/B‑test icons like ad creatives and swap them for seasonal events while the logo stays stable. The role split is taken to the limit: the icon sells the tap, the logo accrues recognition — the same split we cover in how app icons work.

The one shared rule is small‑size readability: a store icon is as tiny as a favicon, and Supercell tests its marks there as ruthlessly as banks do.

What non-game brands can learn

Games are the best at selling an emotion in a second and building a brand around a character. If your product is about joy (kids' goods, food, entertainment), look at game‑grade expressiveness before ordering another minimalist grotesque off the standard list of logo types.
