What Brand Books and Guidelines Are — and Why You Can't Stretch a Logo

The marketer stretched the logo horizontally so it would "fit the header". The contractor printed the mark in purple because "green didn't look right". The office manager put the logo on the bright red background of a greeting card. Everyone meant well — and the brand now looks like three different companies in three places. This is exactly what today's document protects against.

TL;DR

Guidelines are the technical rules of identity use: what you may and may not do with the logo, colors and fonts. A brand book is broader — it adds the brand platform (mission, values, tone of voice). A logobook is narrower: logo rules only. Colloquially it's all called a "brand book", which is fine — what matters is that the document exists.

Three documents everyone confuses

DocumentContentsWho needs it
Logobooklogo rules: versions, clear space, don'tsmicro-business, startups
Guidelines+ colors, fonts, carriers, templatesmost companies
Brand book+ strategy: mission, values, audience, tonemid-size and large business

In practice the names wander. Arguing terminology is pointless — look at the contents.

Inside the guidelines: the key sections

Logo versions

Primary, compact (mark without text), horizontal and vertical lockups, monochrome, dark-background version — each with rules on when to use it. This is where a designer learns the favicon gets the mark, not the full logo with a tagline.

Clear space

The minimum margin around the logo where no text, images or edges may intrude. Usually defined by an element of the mark itself: "the margin equals the height of letter X". Clear space is what separates tidy layouts from "logo glued into the corner".

Minimum size

The threshold below which the logo is illegible: e.g. "no smaller than 24 px digital, 15 mm print". Below it, the compact mark takes over.

Forbidden distortions

The most practical spread of the document — the "don'ts" gallery:

The classic list
  • stretching or squeezing disproportionately;
  • changing brand colors or adding your own;
  • rotating, tilting, mirroring;
  • adding shadows, outlines, gradients, glows;
  • placing the color version on busy or clashing backgrounds;
  • recomposing elements (moving the mark relative to the text);
  • boxing the logo in frames or shapes.

If "stretching it a little" ever seemed harmless: a mark's proportions are part of its recognizability. The eye catches distortion instantly, without even knowing what's wrong.

Colors

The palette with exact codes: HEX and RGB for screens, CMYK and Pantone for print, plus rules on primary color, backgrounds and text contrast. Why colors are fixed as codes, not "by eye" — in our brand colors article.

Fonts

The brand typeface, weights, and substitution rules (what to use in emails and decks where the brand font is unavailable). Without this section, corporate documents drift into ten fonts within six months.

Carriers

Application examples: business cards, letterheads, decks, social media, vehicles, signage, packaging. The more ready templates, the less contractors improvise.

Why small businesses need one

Guidelines seem like corporate toys. Practice says the opposite: small businesses need them more, because contractors rotate faster — a new SMM person, a freelance designer, a discount print shop. Without the document each interprets the style their own way, and a year later the brand has five shades of its color and three logo versions in circulation.

A 5–10 page mini-guideline suffices: logo versions, clear space, sizes, don'ts, color codes, fonts. Studios usually include one in the logo package — that's part of what the studio price tier buys (how much a logo costs).

Where to see good examples

Many companies publish their guidelines: search "brand guidelines" + the name. Google, Telegram, Spotify and others keep public brand-asset pages with logo versions, color codes and usage rules — the best textbook there is.

Building a mini-guideline yourself: an 8-page skeleton

No studio budget? Assemble the document yourself — in Figma, Google Docs or PDF. The proven structure:

  1. Cover — logo, document title, version and date (versions matter: guidelines live and get updated).
  2. Logo versions — full, mark, monochrome, inverse; one sentence each on when to use it.
  3. Clear space and minimum size — a diagram measured via an element of the mark.
  4. The don'ts — 6–8 crossed-out "wrong" images. The most-consulted spread: contractors look at exactly this.
  5. Colors — swatches with HEX/RGB/CMYK. If the colors aren't code-fixed yet — now is the time (how to capture the exact color).
  6. Typography — the brand typeface, weights, and the substitute font for email and decks.
  7. Application examples — 3–4 carriers: business card, site header, avatar, deck.
  8. A contact and a link to the asset folder — so rules and correct files arrive together.

One day of work — and the business gets a document that saves years of correspondence.

Making the guideline actually work (not shelf-sit)

The chronic disease of brand books is that nobody reads them. The countermeasures:

  • A link in every brief. The line "assets and rules: [link]" in every contractor order is the only reliable delivery channel.
  • Files next to rules. A guideline without the folder of correct sources provokes "found the logo on Google". Keep them together (folder organization).
  • A one-page digest. The full document is for designers; for the office manager and SMM — one page: the correct logo, the two main don'ts, the color codes.
  • An annual revision. New carriers appear (dark mode, motion) — the document grows, version and date update.

FAQ

What does a brand book cost? A mini-logobook usually ships within a studio's logo price; full guidelines are separate work comparable to the mark itself; a strategic brand book is agency territory (the price tiers).

Is a brand book legally required? No — it's an internal document. But it helps legally too: it fixes the mark's canon, making deviations easier to qualify as distortion in disputes.

Which comes first — the brand book or the logo? The logo. Guidelines describe an existing identity; ordering "a brand book right away" with no mark is a terminology muddle that usually sells a template.

In short

A brand book/guideline is the identity's user manual: logo versions, clear space, sizes, don'ts, exact colors, fonts. It exists not for beauty but so the brand looks identical across all contractors and media. No guidelines — chaos on a delayed invoice.
