The Telegram Logo History — Where the Paper Plane Came From

The Telegram paper plane is one of the most recognizable marks in messaging: the white silhouette in a blue circle sits on the screens of over a billion users. Remarkably, the mark has barely changed since the 2013 launch — a rare case of a logo nailed on the first try. Let's unpack where the image came from and what's encoded in it.

TL;DR

The Telegram logo is a paper plane — a symbol of a sent message: the folded notes people once launched across classrooms. The mark debuted with the messenger in 2013 and has since seen only cosmetic touch‑ups: the gradient went flat, the plane's tilt and proportions were quietly refined. The current brand blue is roughly #28A8E9, with #0088CC historically used on the web.

Why a paper plane

The messenger was launched by Pavel and Nikolai Durov in August 2013. The name nods to the telegraph and the telegram — the fastest way to deliver text in the pre‑internet era. The visual continuation of that idea is the paper plane — a universal, wordless symbol of a sent message: a folded sheet flying to its recipient.

The image worked for several reasons at once:

  • Language‑independent. A paper plane reads the same in any country — vital for a product with global ambitions.
  • It depicts an action, not an object. Not a phone handset, not an envelope — flight: a message moving toward its recipient. A perfect match for Telegram's core marketing claim: speed.
  • Geometrically simple. The mark is built from a few triangles, so it looks flawless at 16 favicon pixels and on a billboard alike — exactly the case we described in why brands simplify their logos.

The "paper plane = send" icon existed in interfaces before, but it was Telegram that turned it into a global brand: today a paper plane on a Send button reads as a reference to the messenger, not the other way around.

How the mark evolved

2013: launch

The first logo — a white paper plane in a blue circle with a soft vertical gradient and a subtle glossy "lens" — an heir to iOS 6 icon aesthetics, where depth and shine were the norm. The plane points up and to the right, "into flight".

2013-2014: going flat

Within a year of launch, following iOS 7 and the flat‑design wave, the gloss disappeared, the gradient calmed down, the contours got cleaner. The plane's silhouette itself stayed practically untouched.

Later refinements: geometry and color

In the following years the mark was polished almost invisibly: the tilt angle and "wing" balance were trued up, the blue was unified across platforms, monochrome versions were prepared for dark themes and system icons. Telegram never had a "rebrand" in the usual sense — only evolutionary polishing of one and the same image.

Compare that with the Sber story, which packed a full rebrand — new shape, new palette — into the same years: two opposite philosophies of brand life.

The plane beyond the icon

What makes the Telegram logo interesting is how far it travels beyond the app icon:

  • The Send button. Inside the messenger itself, a message is sent by tapping the same plane — a rare case where a product's logo and its core interface element coincide. Every send is a micro‑contact with the brand.
  • Loading animations. The plane flies through splash screens and empty states — the mark "comes alive" without changing shape (the techniques are in animated logos).
  • Stickers and merch. The duck mascot and sticker packs riff on the plane constantly, building the emotional bond strict icon geometry can't.
  • Channels and bots. Thousands of channels build their avatars on a recolored plane — a de facto folk identity of the ecosystem. Starting your own channel? Begin with a logo for a Telegram channel.

The versions that exist

A designer usually needs three variants, and all three live on the Telegram page of our catalog:

  1. The round icon — the plane in a blue circle. For avatars, app lists and anywhere the logo stands in a row of other services.
  2. The bare mark — the plane without a backdrop. For when you control the background: a dark site theme, a brand color, print.
  3. Monochrome — a single‑color silhouette for footers, engraving and documents where color is unavailable or excessive.

A common mistake is stretching the round icon where the bare mark belongs: the circle's white rim on a white background leaves a dirty edge. The right move is to take the backdrop‑free SVG and set the color yourself (a one‑minute job — see how to change a logo's color).

What the paper plane teaches

  1. An action metaphor beats an object metaphor. The plane tells you what the product does — delivers messages fast.
  2. Simple geometry ages slowly. In a decade the mark never looked dated — there was nothing left to simplify.
  3. Consistency beats novelty. While competitors repainted and redrew, Telegram kept compounding recognition of a single silhouette.
