Logo Design Trends 2026 — What's In and What's Out

For ten years every logo was busily going flat — and now the pendulum swings back: gradients have returned, typefaces have character again, and marks are learning to move and change. Here are the main logo trends of 2026 with live examples — and, just as important, an honest list of what already looks like yesterday.

TL;DR

In: gradients and glow (the AI‑product aesthetic), custom typography with character, dynamic and animated marks, pixel and retro nostalgia, simplicity with one memorable detail. Out: sterile look‑alike flat design, random generator‑made geometric marks, fine detail that dies in an app icon.

Context: why the pendulum swung

The 2015-2022 era was defined by total flatness: brand after brand erased gradients, shadows and detail (covered in why brands simplify their logos). Simplification solved the technical problems — marks scale perfectly now — but created a new one: everyone looks the same. Same grotesques, same rounded corners, same palette. In 2026 brands are hunting for individuality without losing the tech advantages. Hence every trend below.

Trend 1. Gradients and glow — the AI aesthetic

The strongest visual shift came from AI products. Look at Gemini, Copilot or ChatGPT: multicolor blends, soft glow, a sense of "living energy". Even the conservative Google added smooth gradient transitions to its G in 2025 — the first change in a decade (details in the Google logo history). In Russia the same language belongs to Sber with its gradient chevron and to GigaChat.

Note: this is not a return to the glossy Web 2.0 gradients with highlights and shadows — the new gradients live on simple, clean forms.

Trend 2. Custom typography with character

The answer to grotesque sameness is type you cannot mistake: accent serifs, deliberate irregularities, ink traps, variable width. Wordmarks are designed as unique lettering again rather than typed in an off‑the‑shelf font. There's a legal push too: custom letters are easier to protect as a trademark. How font choice works and how lettering differs from typesetting: choosing a logo font.

Trend 3. Dynamic and animated marks

A 2026 logo must know how to move: the app splash screen, a response to user action, a living avatar. Marks are designed with an animation scenario from day one — how they appear, breathe, respond. A separate branch is dynamic systems, where the logo changes lawfully from medium to medium (the genre classic being Google's doodles). How to make and where to use an animated version: animated logos.

A pre-order check

Commissioning a logo in 2026, ask the designer up front: "how does this mark animate?" No answer means you're buying a static picture in a world where the main media (phone, stories, splash screen) move.

Trend 4. Pixel and retro nostalgia

The flip side of AI gloss is longing for the "human" digital past: pixel art, early‑OS aesthetics, grain, amateur 90s–00s graphics. Pixel accents appear at fashion brands, game studios and media as a wink to insiders. It's a risky trend: to conservative audiences pixels read as "broken", so they usually live in supporting graphics rather than the primary mark.

Trend 5. Simplicity — with one detail

Minimalism didn't leave; it grew up. The 2026 formula: an extremely simple mark plus one unexpected detail that hooks memory — a hidden symbol, a "wrong" angle, a broken contour. How such details work: logos with hidden meanings. The baseline stands: the mark must survive at 16 pixels — the same size as a favicon — and in monochrome.

What's out

  • Sterile look‑alike flat. A single‑color geometric mark + a neutral grotesque with zero distinguishing features — in 2026 that's not "clean", it's invisible.
  • Generator abstractions. A random geometric shape from a logo builder, connected to the brand by nothing. Telling a meaningful mark from a random one: types of logos.
  • Overloaded detail. Gradients are back; forty‑element crests are not: every 2026 trend is still tested by the app icon — the same platform requirements we cover elsewhere.
  • Blind AI‑aesthetic copying. When a coffee shop, a dental clinic and a law firm all wear gradient glow, the device stops differentiating and starts averaging.

Using trends without regret

  1. A trend is seasoning, not the dish. The mark's core must outlive fashion: type, composition, idea. The trendy layer (gradient, animation) goes on top and is cheaper to replace than the whole logo — we cover what rebranding is and costs separately.
  2. Test on your audience. Pixel nostalgia works great on designers and gamers, poorly on a bank's clientele.
  3. Adjust for brand size. Sber can afford a gradient because it's recognized even in monochrome. A young brand needs recognition first, effects later.
  4. Codify the system. Every trendy device belongs in the brand book with rules: where it applies, where not, and what the static/monochrome fallback looks like.

Visual literacy is the best trend detector: browse the logo catalog and compare how the same device lives across industries. And if you want to experiment with the AI aesthetic hands‑on, start with making a logo with AI.
