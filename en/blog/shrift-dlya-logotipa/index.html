Choosing a Logo Typeface — And Not Getting Fined over the License

Mark, color — and typeface: the three decisions a logo is made of. The typeface is the most underrated of the three: beginners pick "whichever looks pretty", and the company name ends up reading like a kids' party invitation. Plus a legal landmine almost nobody knows about: a font in a logo can bring a claim worth serious money. Let's cover both taste and law.

TL;DR

Type conveys character before meaning is read: sans‑serif — modernity, serif — tradition and status, monospace — engineering, script — personal tone. The rules: one typeface per logo, a 16-pixel test, and crucially — a license that permits logo use: "downloaded for free" and "allowed to use" are different things.

Categories and their character

Sans‑serifs: Helvetica, Inter. Read as "modern, clean, tech". The digital default — which is why everyone looks alike: in a sans world, distinction comes from the mark and the color, as covered in why brands simplify.

Serifs: Times, Playfair. "Tradition, reliability, status" — law, publishing, premium. Risk: serifs clog at small sizes, demanding sturdier weights.

Geometric and wide: Futura, Montserrat and kin. "Confidence, scale". Beloved by developers and banks.

Monospace: JetBrains Mono, IBM Plex Mono. Instant "IT, engineering". Careful: already a startup cliché.

Script and display: calligraphy, lettering, decorative faces. Maximum character, maximum risk: legibility drops, taste betrays easily, and at 16 pixels flourishes turn to mush. They work when the lettering is the logo, drawn by a professional.

Choosing: process over taste

  1. From meaning. Return to your one‑sentence brand character (see the DIY logo guide) and translate it into a category: "reliable and human" — humanist sans; "expensive and eternal" — serif.
  2. From the market category. Study competitors' type: full match is bad, full rupture too. Category code + one difference.
  3. The size test. Set the name at three sizes: large (signage), medium (site header), small (16–24 px). The face must survive all three.
  4. The letter‑pair test. Check your name specifically: awkward pairs, digits, the ampersand, Latin and Cyrillic versions. A pretty alphabet ≠ your word looking good.
  5. Refinement. Logo text is graphics, not typed text: kern manually and convert to outlines.
The one-typeface rule

One typeface per logo, at most a weight game (bold + light). A descriptor ("studio", "clinic") may take a second, neutral face. Three fonts in a logo doesn't happen — three fonts in a DIY job does.

Licensing: the most expensive rookie mistake

A font is software, an object of copyright. "Downloaded free from a font site" only means the file cost nothing — the license defines usage rights, with tiers: personal use only, desktop, web, and for logos — commercial use, sometimes with a separate logo‑use clause.

Claims from type foundries aren't a scare story but routine practice: usage monitoring, then a letter demanding a retroactive license or compensation. For logos the risk is special: the mark propagates across all media, and migrating off an unlicensed font means changing the whole identity.

The safe options
  • Open Font License (OFL): Google Fonts — thousands of faces, commercial and logo use permitted. Strong Cyrillic options: Inter, Manrope, Golos Text, the PT family, Onest, Unbounded.
  • A purchased license of the right scope from a foundry — for uniqueness and full coverage.
  • Custom lettering — letters drawn for you, rights transferred by contract: the "expensive and forever" option.
Check right now

If your logo already exists — find out which font it uses (ask the designer or identify via WhatTheFont) and what license it carries. "The designer did it, I don't know" is the most common answer and the most common time bomb.

How to use the table: pick two‑three candidates by character, set your name in them and test at three sizes. And remember that popularity is uniqueness's flip side: every designer recognizes Inter in a logo. For distinction, either modify letters (a cut, a ligature, one altered form) or save up for custom type.

Modifying a typeface to make the logo yours

Between "just typed it" and "commissioned lettering" lies the middle path — modification. The classic moves:

  1. A cut or break in one letter. One non‑standard letter turns type into a mark: a sliced leg of the K, a broken O. Less is more: one modification, not five.
  2. A ligature. Joining two neighbors with a shared stroke — compactness plus uniqueness.
  3. A letter swapped for a symbol. The O as a ring/dial/bean per the business. Careful: the move is worn; the symbol must surprise.
  4. Manual kerning. Even without modifications, adjusting pairs (especially around T, Y, A) separates crafted work from default typing.
  5. Outline conversion and contour editing: corner rounding, proportion shifts — done in the curve editor, not the font file.
Modification ≠ license bypass

Altered letters remain derivative of the typeface: the source license still applies. Modify only faces whose license permits it (OFL does), and don't "slightly change" a pirated font hoping nobody notices: foundries notice.

Outlining: the mandatory finale

A finished logo must not depend on an installed font: convert text to outlines so the logo becomes pure shapes. This solves three problems at once: the file opens identically for every vendor, accidental font substitution becomes impossible, and license‑wise outlined curves are usually the safest mode of font use (still, read your specific license). Keep the live‑text source separately for future edits.

In short

A typeface is brand character written in letters: choose from meaning and category, test on your own name at 16 pixels, stick to one face. And never build a logo on a font whose license you haven't read: Google Fonts under OFL is the safe harbor; "a pretty font from a listicle" is a lottery with expensive tickets.
