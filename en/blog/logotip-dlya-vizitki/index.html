A Logo for a Business Card — How to Prepare the Mark for Print

A business card is a tiny but important brand carrier: it's held in the hand, examined up close, and any logo flaw is immediately visible. On a website a flaw can be blamed on the screen, but on paper a person studies point‑blank there's nothing to hide. And the whole difficulty is that a business card lives by the rules of print, not a screen: what looked great on a monitor may "blur," shift in color, or get cut off on paper. So preparing a logo for a business card must start from exactly these rules.

How printing a card differs from a screen

To understand what exactly to prepare, let's start with how printing differs from a screen — and there are four differences here, each with its own consequence. A screen glows and works in RGB; a print shop applies ink and works in CMYK. Hence the first: resolution. A screen makes do with 72 dpi, while print needs at least 300 dpi, or the mark will come out with "jaggies" at the edges. The second — color: a bright on‑screen RGB color shifts in CMYK print, and blue can "go" purple (more in a logo for print). The third — trim: a card is cut from a large sheet, and the cut line "wanders" by fractions of a millimeter, so bleed is needed. And the fourth — size: thin lines and small text readable on a monitor may vanish entirely on paper at small scale.

It's worth seeing how this looks in practice, because abstract "differences" are easy to underestimate. A logo with hairlines and a soft transition looks elegant on a monitor, but the same card, printed at small scale, renders those lines as either gray mush or white gaps, and the "brand" bright blue on matte paper dims and drifts toward steel. Worst of all, this becomes noticeable only on the production run — when five hundred cards are in the box and can't be fixed. So all the requirements below aren't over‑caution but a way to see these flaws on screen in advance rather than on paper after the fact.

Logo file requirements

From these differences the file requirements grow. Since print needs crispness at any enlargement, the ideal is vector: SVG, AI, or EPS print crisply at any size (and if you only have a PNG, it has to be converted to vector or taken at 300 dpi and up). Since color shifts in print, the logo is converted to CMYK in advance and checked for whether the brand shades "drifted" Since the print shop may not have your font, the mark's text is converted to outlines so it doesn't substitute another. And since printing can be single‑color or embossed, a flat single-color version is prepared — it'll be needed where color isn't transferred at all.

Margins, bleed, and clear space

Three things most often ruin a card, and all are about the space around the mark. Bleed — the background and elements should extend past the cut line by 2-3 mm, or a white stripe appears along the edge where the blade passed a bit off. Safe area — important elements, the logo and text, are kept away from the edge so they don't get trimmed. And logo clear space is the empty space around the mark itself that text and the edge don't intrude into: without it the card looks cramped and sloppy, even if the logo itself is perfect.

How to place a logo on a business card

When the file is ready, the question of composition remains, and it's solved by the same "a card is examined up close" principle. Don't shrink it too much: the logo should be readable but not overpower the contacts — and if the mark is complex, use its simplified version, as in an adaptive logo, because small details vanish on paper anyway. Give the mark air: clear space matters more than "fitting more in," and a logo pressed to the edge always looks cheaper than one with space around it. Check the background: if the card is colored or dark, a light version of the mark is needed — the same principle as in a logo for dark mode — and a transparent background is a must, or a white rectangle appears around the logo. And keep a unified style: the card's colors and font must match the rest of the brand, because a card is as much a consistency touchpoint as a website or an email.

A checklist before sending to the print shop

It's convenient to gather all this into a short check before sending to print. Is the logo in vector or at 300 dpi and up? Are colors converted to CMYK and checked? Is the font converted to outlines? Are bleed and safe areas allowed for? Is there a single-color and, if needed, a light version? Is clear space around the mark respected? If even one item is "no" — the card isn't ready for print yet, and it's better to fix this now than on the production run. What makes a logo suitable for any medium at all is covered in the foundational article what a logo is.

The short takeaway

A logo for a business card lives by print rules, not screen rules, and that's exactly why you can't just "take it from the website": paper is examined up close, and any flaw is immediately visible. From the four differences between print and screen — resolution, color, trim, and small size — all the requirements grow: vector or high resolution, the CMYK color model, font in outlines, bleed, safe areas, and clear space around the mark, plus mandatory single-color and, for colored cards, light versions. Check colors in CMYK in advance — on‑screen brightness looks different on paper — and keep the card's style unified with the rest of the brand.
