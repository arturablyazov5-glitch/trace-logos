Famous Brand Colors — HEX Codes and Where to Find Official Ones

Yandex yellow, Sber green, VK blue — we recognize these colors before we manage to read the name. That's no accident: the brain reads color faster than shape and text, and brands know it — which is why they spend millions to claim a shade and pin it down to the exact code. Then a designer makes a "pay via SBP" button in a "roughly this" purple, and the eye stumbles, though everything seems in place. It stumbles precisely because a brand color isn't decoration but an asset with an exact number, and you can't hit it "by eye."

TL;DR

Every major brand has its color fixed in the brand book with an exact HEX code. A "similar" shade is a bad idea for two reasons at once: recognition drops and the color's legal protection collapses. Exact codes come from official guidelines or straight from the SVG logo — never from an eyedropper on a search‑result image.

Shades change with redesigns

These codes are a snapshot for today, and they don't stay constant. Brands update palettes: "VK blue" before and after the 2021 rebrand are different colors, and Meta's blue was refined too. Doing something official — check the brand's current guideline: a five‑year‑old article will mislead you.

Why "by eye" is expensive

With the codes in front of you, the question arises: does precision really matter if the shade almost matched? It does, and here are three reasons, each of which turns "almost" into a real loss.

The first is recognition. It rests exactly on the accuracy of the hit: the instant "oh, that's Sber" fires before the name is read only if the green is the right one. Shift the shade a little and recognition slows — and with it goes the whole point for which the brand claimed the color.

The second reason is graver than recognition: legal protection. A color can be part of a trademark, and then a "roughly this" shade is no longer an aesthetic but a legal problem. Sber registered its green, T-Bank its yellow, precisely so a competitor couldn't mimic them by color. Using someone else's brand shade in your own mark in the same category is a direct risk; where the line of the permissible runs is covered in can you use someone else's logo.

And the third is consistency. When a company has hundreds of touchpoints — site, app, ads, signage — only an exact code guarantees the green on a billboard and the green in the app are one and the same. Let the color go "by eye" on even one carrier, and the system starts to fray.

All three lead to one practical conclusion: color shouldn't be picked — it should be taken from a reliable source.

Where to get any brand's official color

There are essentially three sources, ordered by descending reliability:

  1. Brand guidelines. Search "brand guidelines" plus the name. Google, Telegram, Spotify have public pages with exact codes and usage rules. This is the primary source, maximum trust.
  2. The SVG logo. If there's no guideline, the color is written right in the file's code as a fill attribute — a source without distortion: this is the original itself, while copies and re‑shoots don't count. How to read the code from a file is covered in how to find a logo's color.
  3. Press kits. Most companies have a "for press" page with logos and colors — a backup but reliable option.

And there's exactly one method that looks simplest and therefore most tempting — and it's the one that gives the wrong result.

How not to do it

An eyedropper on a logo from image search. There you'll find JPGs with compression artifacts, re‑encoded copies and outright fakes. You'll grab #20A23C instead of #21A038 — seems close, yet next to the real one it looks dirty. An eyedropper is honest only as far as the file beneath it — and a file from search is almost always distorted.

How to use others' colors legally

A color's protected status doesn't forbid touching it at all. Showing a brand in its own color is fine: a "Log in with VK" button in blue #0077FF, a list of banks with their brand icons, a review of services with their palettes. This is the same nominative (naming) use as with logos: you point at the brand while remaining yourself. What's forbidden is building your own identity on someone else's protected color in the same product category. A yellow bank, a green bank and a red carrier are already taken, and stepping onto that ground ends in a lawsuit.

Since we've mentioned that a color can be "taken," it's worth understanding how a color gets claimed legally at all.

Can you "patent" a color

Yes, and such registrations stopped being exotica long ago. A color (more precisely, a color designation) is registered as a trademark if its distinctiveness is proven: consumers reliably tie the shade to that specific brand. The global classics — Milka purple, Tiffany blue, Louboutin's red sole. In Russia, Sberbank green and T-Bank yellow are registered; that's why "the yellow bank" in a competitor's ad reads as a legal risk.

But this protection has a hard boundary that removes half the fears: it applies only within a specific product category. Green as such belongs to no one — green in banking services does. A florist can freely be green; a new bank cannot. And the bar of proof is high: color is registered by a handful of brands with years of history and recognition surveys — a young company simply won't be granted such a mark.

Dark mode: a color now has a second version

There's one more reason a single HEX stopped covering every case — dark interfaces. A color tuned for a white background behaves differently on black: a saturated blue "vibrates," a dark green sinks, a yellow starts to shout. So mature design systems keep two palette versions: for light mode — the base codes from the brand book, for dark — adjusted ones, usually a touch lighter and less saturated.

For anyone using others' brand colors in their own interface, the takeaway is direct: check "Log in with…" buttons and bank icons in both themes. An official color on a non‑native background may need a backing plate — which is exactly why payment‑system guidelines describe in such detail which version of the badge to place on which background.

Common questions

Why didn't the color from the official site match the brand book? Three reasons: a fresh redesign hasn't reached the site yet; you sampled the color with an eyedropper on a JPG (distortion); the designers deliberately use an "interface" version of the color, different from print. Trust order: fresh brand book → SVG logo → website.

How to find a brand's color without public guidelines? The SVG logo is the most reliable source: the color is written in the file's code. Download the vector and check fill — how exactly is shown in how to find a logo's color.

How many colors are in a brand palette? The core is 1–2 brand colors, plus functional ones: 2–4 neutrals (text, backgrounds) and semantics (success/error/warning). Palettes of ten "brand" colors show up in ecosystems where each service needs its own accent.

What to remember

A brand color is as much a brand asset as the logo, and it always has an exact code — because recognition, legal protection and consistency all rest on that precision. Hence the single rule: take the color from the primary source — a guideline or the SVG — don't pick it by eye and don't sample it from a search image.
