Famous Brand Colors — HEX Codes and Where to Find Official Ones

Yandex yellow, Sber green, VK blue — we recognize these colors faster than the logos themselves. Companies spend millions to claim a shade and pin it down to an exact code. And then a designer ships a payment button in "roughly that" purple — and the eye stumbles. Here are the official colors of popular brands, and how to find such codes yourself.

TL;DR

Every major brand fixes its color in a brand book with an exact HEX code. Using a "similar" shade is a bad idea: recognition drops and attentive viewers sense the fake. Exact codes come from official guidelines or straight from the SVG logo.

Shades change with redesigns

Brands refresh palettes: VK blue before and after the 2021 rebrand are different colors; Meta's blue was adjusted too. For anything official, check the brand's current guidelines, not a five-year-old article.

Why brands pin color down to a code

Recognition. Marketing research consistently shows color is the first thing the brain reads in a brand — before shape and text. Hitting the exact shade means an instant "oh, that's Sber" before the name is even read.

Legal protection. Color can be part of a trademark. Sber registered its green and T-Bank its yellow precisely so competitors can't mimic them. Building on someone else's protected color in the same category is a risk — more in can you use someone else's logo.

Consistency. With hundreds of touchpoints — site, app, ads, offices — only an exact code guarantees the green is the same green everywhere.

Where to get any brand's official color

  1. Brand guidelines. Search "brand guidelines" + the name. Google, Telegram and Spotify keep public pages with exact codes and usage rules.
  2. The SVG logo. The color sits in the file's fill attribute — a distortion-free source. How to read it: how to find a logo's color.
  3. Press kits. Most companies have a press page with logos and colors.
How not to do it

An eyedropper on a logo from image search. That's JPGs with compression artifacts, re-encoded copies and outright fakes. You'll get #20A23C instead of #21A038 — close, yet visibly dirty next to the real thing.

Using other brands' colors legally

Showing a brand in its own color is fine: a "Sign in with VK" button in #0077FF, a bank list with branded icons, a service review with their palettes. It's the same nominative use as with logos. What you can't do is build your own identity on someone else's protected color in the same product category — the yellow bank, the green bank and the red carrier are taken.

Can a color really be "patented"?

Yes — and it's not legal exotica. A color can be registered as a trademark once its distinctiveness is proven: consumers steadily associate the shade with that specific brand. The world classics: Milka purple, Tiffany blue, the Louboutin red sole. In Russia, Sberbank's green and T-Bank's yellow are registered — which is why "the yellow bank" in a competitor's ad is a risk, not a figure of speech.

The boundaries matter: protection applies within a product category. Green as such belongs to nobody — green in banking services does. A florist can freely be green; a new bank cannot. And the evidence bar is high: only brands with years of history and recognition surveys get color marks — a young company won't.

Dark mode: brand colors' second life

With dark interfaces everywhere, brands hit a new problem: a color tuned for white behaves differently on black. Saturated blue "vibrates" on dark, deep green sinks, yellow starts shouting. Mature design systems therefore keep two palette versions: light theme — the brand book codes; dark theme — adjusted ones (usually slightly lighter and less saturated).

The practical takeaway when using other brands' colors in your UI: check the "Sign in with…" buttons and bank icons in both themes. An official color on a foreign background may need a backing plate — which is exactly why payment-system guidelines describe so insistently which badge version goes on which background.

FAQ

Why doesn't the color from the official site match the brand book? Three options: the site hasn't caught up with a redesign; you eyedropped a JPG (distortion); or the site designers deliberately use an "interface" variant of the color distinct from print. Trust order: fresh brand book → SVG logo → website.

How do I get the color of a brand with no public guidelines? The SVG logo is the most reliable source: the color is written in the file. Download the vector and read the fill (how).

How many colors does a brand palette hold? The core is 1–2 brand colors, plus functional ones: 2–4 neutrals (text, backgrounds) and semantic colors (success/error/warning). Ten-color "brand" palettes belong to ecosystems where each service needs its own accent.

In short

A brand color is as much an asset as the logo, and it always has an exact code. Take it from guidelines or the SVG, never by eye, and never from an eyedropper on a JPG.
