AI‑Generated Logos — An Honest Look at When It Works and When It Doesn't

"Why pay a designer when AI draws a logo in a minute?" — the question every new founder asks in 2026. The answer is less simple than generator ads promise. Let's take a sober look: what AI actually delivers, where the traps hide, and how to extract real value without stepping on rakes.

TL;DR

AI is a great idea generator: 30 directions in an evening, free. But the output has three systemic problems: no vector (it's a picture, not a logo), no uniqueness (similar prompts produce similar marks) and murky copyright. The working scheme: ideas from AI, final vector redraw and trademark check from a human.

What AI does well

Iteration speed. A designer shows 3–5 concepts in a week; a generator, 50 in an evening. Priceless for brainstorming — you quickly learn whether to pursue a mark, a monogram, a mascot or lettering.

Style experiments. "Same mark but minimalist / hand‑lettered / 70s style" — iterations that would take a human days.

Cheap start. For a pet project, a hypothesis test or a 100-subscriber channel, a generated mark is an honest choice. Not every project needs six‑figure branding.

The three systemic problems

1. You get a picture, not a logo

Image generators output raster — PNG or JPG. A logo, as covered in our format guide, must exist in vector: otherwise no print, no signage, no crisp favicon. Dedicated logo generators do output vectors, but often as curves with hundreds of stray points — miserable to edit.

Plus the genre classic: neural networks still garble letters. Your company name easily becomes "Comqany" — every wordmark needs manual re‑typesetting.

2. Uniqueness was never on offer

The model is trained on millions of existing logos and outputs the statistically probable — i.e., similar to everything at once. Two founders prompting "minimalist coffee shop logo, bean, brown" get sibling images. For a business that's a legal problem, not an aesthetic one: if the mark collides with a registered trademark, a rejected registration is the good outcome; a lawsuit is the bad one.

3. Copyright is a gray zone

Under most jurisdictions (Russia included), only a human can be an author. A purely generated image with no human creative contribution risks having no copyright protection at all — anyone could use it, and defending it would be hard. Service terms differ too: some transfer rights, some keep a license, some free tiers allow non‑commercial use only. Read the specific generator's ToS before business use — and better, rework the result by hand, creating that human contribution.

The working scheme: AI + human

  1. Generate directions. Collect 30–50 variants across styles. Don't hunt for a finished logo — hunt for good moves: a shape, a composition, a metaphor.
  2. Select and check. Pick 2–3 directions and run them through trademark databases and reverse image search. Something similar registered in your class? Bin the direction.
  3. Redraw in vector. A designer — or you, in Figma/Inkscape — redraws the mark cleanly: smooth curves, measured spacing, a real typeface instead of AI letters. This is the creative contribution that makes the mark yours.
  4. The file kit. SVG, transparent PNG, dark‑background versions — the standard set without which a logo isn't a logo.
A trick for visual literacy

Before generating, study how strong brands in your category solved their logos: shapes, color counts, mark vs lettering. Our logo catalog lets you browse by industry — banks, IT, delivery, auto. Thirty minutes of looking sharply improves your prompts.

Writing logo prompts: specifics instead of "make it pretty"

Generation quality is 80% prompt. The skeleton of a working one:

  1. Mark type: "minimalist icon logo" / "lettermark of letter K" / "mascot logo". Skip it and the model chooses — usually an overloaded emblem.
  2. Object and metaphor: not "coffee shop logo" but "a coffee bean merging into a sunrise". A concrete metaphor is the main lever.
  3. Style and constraints: "flat, geometric, two colors, white background, no text". The text ban matters: models draw garbage letters, and you'll re‑typeset anyway.
  4. An era/school reference (optional): "in the style of 1970s geometric logos", "swiss design".

An assembled example: minimalist flat logo icon, a coffee bean merging into a sunrise, geometric shapes, two colors — terracotta and cream, white background, no text, no gradients.

Then iterate: generate 8–12 variants, keep the good move, refine the prompt ("same, but the bean larger, no rays") and repeat. Expecting a masterpiece from the first query is the top cause of generator disappointment.

The uniqueness check: how to actually search

"Check the databases" sounds abstract — here's the concrete version:

  • Reverse image search: feed the chosen variant to Google Images and Yandex. Similar marks in the results are a warning (note their category).
  • Trademark registries: open searches over registered marks and filed applications, by image and by description (class + keywords).
  • A full attorney‑run search — mandatory before registering: paid databases see pending applications missing from public ones. The whole registration machine: the trademark article.

And remember: an "inspired‑by" match is also a risk. If your generated fox suspiciously resembles a famous browser's fox — different category, but the reputational aftertaste and a redo are likely.

The acceptance checklist for a generated logo

Before declaring it done, run the list:

  • [ ] Redrawn cleanly in vector (not raw auto‑tracing)
  • [ ] Text set in a real typeface with a verified license (see the fonts article)
  • [ ] Works in monochrome and inverted on dark
  • [ ] Recognizable at 16–48 pixels
  • [ ] Reverse image search finds no twins
  • [ ] The full file kit exists: SVG, transparent PNG, dark‑background versions
  • [ ] The generator's ToS permits commercial use

Seven checkmarks — and a generated logo is no worse than a "human" one of the same price class. The skipped items are exactly the difference studios charge for.

In short

In 2026, AI is a brilliant sketchbook and a poor finisher. Use it for speed and breadth of ideas, then finish like an adult: uniqueness check, manual vector redraw, full file kit. A logo lives for years; an hour saved today shouldn't cost a rebrand tomorrow.
