Brand Architecture — One Logo or a Family of Marks

A company launches a new product — and faces a fork easy to slip past unnoticed. Give the product its own name and logo — or release it under the parent brand, adding a suffix? This choice affects not only the design but how the audience will understand the whole lineup. Get it wrong, and the customer gets confused: is this the same brand, a different one, or a competitor entirely?

This question is about brand architecture. It sounds like something for corporations, but the "one mark or a family of marks" fork faces any growing business that gets a second product. Let's break down the main models and how to choose yours.

What brand architecture is

Brand architecture is a system describing how all of a company's brands, sub‑brands, and products relate: who has their own logo, who inherits the parent's, who exists separately altogether. Essentially it's the answer to "how many brands do we have and how do they relate to each other"

The right architecture helps the audience instantly understand what belongs to what. The wrong one breeds confusion and scatters the recognizability a brand built over years.

Two extremes: house of brands and branded house

All models fall between two poles.

A branded house — one strong parent brand, with all products under it with suffixes. The logo is the same everywhere, only the line name changes. The plus — all recognizability works for each product; the minus — if one product stumbles, the shadow falls on the whole brand. This is consistency territory: one mark, one design system.

A house of brands — a parent company owns many independent brands, each with its own logo and face, and the link to the "parent" is often not advertised at all. The plus — brands don't drag each other down and hit different audiences; the minus — each has to be promoted from scratch, recognizability doesn't carry over.

Between them are hybrids: sub‑brands that have both their own individuality and a visible link to the parent mark.

When a new product needs its own logo

Decide not by taste but by questions:

  • Same audience and promise? If the new product is for the same people and about the same thing — usually the parent mark with a suffix suffices. Different audiences and values are a reason for a separate face.
  • Won't one product drag down the other? If a failure or scandal in the new direction could hit the main brand, it's wiser to separate them.
  • Is there resource? A separate brand means separate marketing from scratch. No budget to promote a second name — stay under the parent.
  • Won't it confuse the customer? The main criterion: the audience must instantly understand what they're looking at. If a new name adds confusion rather than clarity — that's an architecture mistake, not just a design one.
Ecosystems are a common sub-brand case

Large tech ecosystems usually build exactly a family of sub‑brands: a single parent "umbrella" mark plus recognizable service logos tied by a common style. This way each service has its own face, but at a glance it's clear whose "family" it belongs to. It's a hybrid: product individuality on the foundation of a shared design system.

How marks in a family are tied together

If you chose sub‑brands, their logos need to be "related" somehow, or the family falls apart into random marks. They're usually tied through:

  • A common element — a recurring symbol, shape, or "umbrella" mark.
  • A unified color logic — a palette recognizable across the whole lineup.
  • A common font and style — the same lettering of names.
  • Unified rules — all fixed in the brand book and design system.

In spirit it's close to a dynamic logo: an unchanging core plus variations. Only here it's not states of one mark that vary but different products of one family.

The main thing

Brand architecture is the system of links between a company's brands, sub‑brands, and products, and it answers "do we have one mark or a family of marks" The extremes are a branded house (one logo for all, shared recognizability but shared risk) and a house of brands (each its own face, flexible but promoted from scratch); between them are sub‑brands, the most common choice of growing companies and ecosystems. Decide whether a new product needs its own mark not by taste but by questions about audience, risks, resources, and — most importantly — whether it will confuse the customer. And if you chose a family of marks, tie them with a common element, color, and font, fixing the rules in a design system.
