How to Make a Logo Yourself for Free — An Honest Step‑by‑Step Guide

No budget for a designer, generators churn out templates, and you need a logo. Good news: a decent starter logo is genuinely doable yourself, for free, in an evening or two — if you follow the right process instead of drawing "something pretty" at random. Bad news: DIY logos have typical failures that give them away at first glance. Let's walk the process so you avoid them.

TL;DR

The starter‑logo formula: a simple mark or a clean wordmark + one color + one typeface. Tools: Figma (free) or Inkscape. Process: meaning → visual research → paper sketches → vector drawing → small‑size test → file kit. Don't aim for "expensive and complex" — complexity is exactly what betrays a DIY job.

Step 0Accept that the logo isn't the main thing

The liberating truth: at business start, the logo matters less than it seems. It has to be neat and stay out of the way. Recognition comes from the product, while the mark just accompanies it on a business card and in the site header. So the goal reads "clean and professional", and genius gets ordered with your first million.

Step 1State the meaning in one sentence

Before opening any editor, write down: what the company does, for whom, and the one feeling the mark should leave (reliability? speed? care? boldness?). One, not five. Check every later decision — shape, color, type — against that sentence. This is the work studios charge for; yours takes half an hour and saves ten rounds of redoing.

Step 2Do visual research (30 minutes)

Look at 30–50 logos in your industry: what's common, how many colors, mark or wordmark, which shapes. Note patterns instead of likes and dislikes: banks favor geometry and blue/green, delivery favors motion, beauty favors lettering. Browsing by category is easy in our logo catalog — banks, IT, auto, delivery, all in one place.

Write down what you see in three columns: shape, color, typeface. After thirty logos the table shows the category norm and the open spots in it by itself — that nobody in your niche uses green, say, or that everyone took a circle. That table later answers the "why this color" question in place of a reference to taste.

Your goal is to fit the category and differ by one detail; the reverse order produces a mark that looks foreign in its own industry.

Step 3Pick the logo type

Three are realistic solo:

  • Wordmark: the name in a clean typeface, perhaps with one modification (a cut letter, a ligature, a colored dot). The safest path — hard to ruin.
  • Monogram: 1–2 letters in a simple shape (circle, square, shield). Great for long names.
  • Simple mark + text: a geometric symbol beside the name. Riskier: the symbol has to be right.

Mascots, complex illustrations, 3D gradient emblems — not today's difficulty level. As covered in why brands simplify, even giants fled complexity — it's no longer a status marker.

Step 4Paper sketches (before the computer!)

10–20 quick 30-second pencil sketches. Paper generates options far faster, and your hand doesn't cling to editor features. Keep 2–3 live directions.

Step 5Draw it in vector

Tools: Figma (browser, free) or Inkscape (offline, free). Canva won't do for finals: it exports vector only on paid plans. Photoshop is out too, being raster (why that's bad is explained in our vector vs raster piece).

The drawing rules that separate "DIY" from "DIY but you can't tell":

  1. Build from geometry. Circles, rectangles, boolean operations. Keep the pen for whatever geometry cannot assemble. Geometry gives the evenness the eye reads as professionalism.
  2. One typeface. A good sans with a free license (Inter, Manrope). No "beautiful" decorative fonts from listicles — DIY marker #1.
  3. One color plus black/white. Chosen for the meaning from step 1 and category norms. Two colors max. Rainbow gradients — no.
  4. Alignment and spacing. Turn on the grid and place elements along the guides: eyeballing misses by a couple of pixels, and that is enough. Crooked spacing — DIY marker #2.

What to do when nothing comes

A sketching block is an ordinary part of the process, and you get out of it mechanically. First move: take the first letter of the name and drop it into a simple shape, a circle or a rounded square. Then change one variable at a time — the letter slant, the stroke weight, a cut in the shape, an offset from the center. Twenty options take half an hour, and one of them usually works.

Second move: list ten objects directly tied to the keyword from step 1. Sunrise gives a semicircle, a horizon, an arc; delivery gives an arrow, a box, a route. Draw the simplest object on the list: the interesting one eats an evening and still fails to read at 16 pixels.

Step 6Stress‑test it

  • The 16-pixel test: shrink to favicon size. Recognizable? Small text may vanish, but the mark must survive.
  • The monochrome test: one color only. Works without color — the shape is strong.
  • The background test: white, black and your brand color.
  • The stranger test: show three people for 3 seconds; ask what they remember.
  • The memory test: a day later, draw your own mark from memory with a pencil. Five lines was enough — the shape is as simple as it needs to be. Had to recall details — those are the details to drop.
  • The uniqueness test: reverse image search — do you collide with an existing brand? A collision costs you: it is awkward and legally dangerous at once.

Step 7The file kit

Assemble the standard set: SVG (primary), transparent PNG at 1000+ px, monochrome versions, a dark‑background version. What each is for — in the format guide. Guard the Figma/Inkscape source file — it's your master.

Name the files right away and by one scheme: brand-logo-full.svg, brand-logo-mark.svg, brand-logo-white.png. In six months you will be emailing them to a contractor, and "logo final final2.png" comes back stretched inside someone else's deck. Put the whole folder in cloud storage with a shared link so the file never has to be dug out of a mail thread.

When DIY is a bad idea

Franchising, an investment round, retail entry, trademark registration — situations where the mark faces professional and legal scrutiny. There, saving on design costs more than the saving: see how much a logo costs and what you pay for.

A worked example: the "Sunrise" coffee shop logo

Let's run the process on a concrete case so the steps stop being abstract.

Step 1, meaning: "A morning coffee shop by an office district; the one feeling — a warm, energizing start of the day."

Step 2, research: half an hour in the food/coffee category — the patterns emerge: beans, cups, steam; warm browns and terracotta; lots of lettering. Conclusion: skip the bean and the cup (worn out), take the sunrise metaphor.

Step 3, type: mark + text: a semicircle sun rising from a "saucer" line, the name beside it in a geometric sans.

Steps 4–5, sketches and drawing: paper reveals that a sun with rays becomes a hedgehog at 16 pixels — the rays go, leaving a clean semicircle with one steam arc. In Figma: the semicircle = circle minus rectangle (boolean), the arc — a circle segment with rounded caps. Color — terracotta #C8552D (warm, free in the category per our map), typeface — Manrope SemiBold.

Step 6, tests: the semicircle reads at 16 px; monochrome works; on dark, terracotta swaps to light beige. Reverse image search finds no twins.

Step 7, the kit: SVG, 1024 PNG, a white version, the standalone mark for avatars. Total: two evenings and zero budget.

Note the case's core trick: every decision (dropping the rays, the color, the typeface) follows from a test or the category map. "I like it this way" never appears on that list, and that process is what separates a deliberate DIY from a random one.

Five DIY giveaways and how to avoid them

Look at your result through a designer's eyes. The mark betrays itself if:

  1. A library clipart icon + the name. Free‑set icons are recognizable to professionals at a glance and legally non‑unique. Build even simple shapes yourself from geometry.
  2. Outlines instead of fills everywhere. Thin line‑marks are a fashion that dies at small sizes and in print. Beginners — use fills.
  3. Effects: shadow, three‑stop gradient, glow. Remove all of it. A flat shape reads as professional, and why even the giants simplified is covered separately.
  4. Text placed "by eye" next to the mark. The gap should be systematic: usually the letter height or half the mark. Measure, don't guess.
  5. Two typefaces and three colors. The starter formula is iron: one typeface, one color plus black/white.

After the logo: the minimal identity launch

The logo is done — spend one more evening making it work as a system:

  • Assemble the file kit and one cloud folder.
  • Build the favicon and avatars from the mark.
  • Write one page: color codes, the typeface name, "no stretching, no recoloring" — your micro‑guideline; we explain why it matters separately.
  • Check the name and mark against trademark registries — before printing the signage; we cover how separately.

In short

Meaning → research → paper sketches → geometric drawing in Figma → tests (16 px, monochrome, backgrounds) → file kit. Stay simple: one typeface, one color, clean geometry. Such a logo won't win awards, but it will honestly do its job until the business grows into a studio budget.
