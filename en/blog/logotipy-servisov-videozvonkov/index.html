Video Call Service Logos — Zoom, Teams, Meet, Discord

In 2020 video calls went from a corporate niche to humanity's main way of talking within a month — and this category's marks suddenly appeared on every screen on the planet. The genre cliché is obvious: the video camera pictogram. What's interesting is who uses it, who hides it, and who built the brand on something else entirely. Here are the logos of Zoom, Teams, Google Meet, Discord, FaceTime and Yandex Telemost.

TL;DR

The category's three strategies: the camera as the mark (FaceTime, Google Meet), a letter + people (Zoom with a wordmark, Teams with a "T" and silhouettes) and a mascot instead of a camera (Discord). The dominant color is blue: the category grew out of the corporate world. Files are in the Video Calls category.

Zoom: the word that became a verb

Zoom wears the category's simplest mark: a blue geometric wordmark, with a white camera on blue in the icon. The brand's secret is not the graphics but the name: the short, punchy "zoom" became a verb ("let's zoom") — and once a name turns into a verb, the logo's job is to stay out of the way. The blue is the standard corporate trust code straight out of logo color psychology.

Tellingly, the brand survived the explosive growth of 2020 without a redesign: a mark designed "not to interfere" scaled from a niche b2b service to a billion users without a single edit — a living case study in when NOT to change a logo.

Teams and Meet: ecosystem marks

Microsoft Teams — a purple "T" with human silhouettes. The purple is no accident: in Microsoft's palette blue belongs to Word and Outlook, green to Excel, so Teams took a free sector. That is ecosystem logic: product marks differ by color within one shared style — just like Google's and Yandex's office suites.

Google Meet — a camera folded from Google's signature four colors. The move is the inverse of Microsoft's: not one color per product but the whole company palette in every product — Gmail, Drive and Meet differ in shape, never in color — the same system covered in the Google logo history.

FaceTime — the benchmark system pictogram: a white camera on green, the same green as Apple's phone icon. The mark says: a video call is just a call. Utilitarian honesty in the spirit of system app icons.

Discord: the gaming outsider

Discord is the only one in the category to build its mark on a character: the smiling gamepad "Clyde", a classic mascot. The reason is its origin: the service was born as voice chat for gamers, and a friendly mascot set it apart from the corporate gravity of Skype and Teams. When the pandemic pushed Discord toward a mainstream audience, the 2021 rebrand simplified Clyde and changed the type — but kept the mascot: it remained the main asset.

Telemost and the rest

Yandex Telemost is a sub‑brand inside the parent Yandex system; Google Voice is a handset in the Google palette. Both confirm the rule: inside a strong ecosystem a product needs no unique symbol — the system style and a word suffice.

The curious takeaway: the pure camera pictogram survived only in system apps (FaceTime, Meet). Standalone brands either hide it behind a word (Zoom) or drop it entirely (Discord): a camera means the function, while a brand must mean the difference.

What to take away

If your product lives inside an ecosystem — inherit the system, don't invent a symbol. If you stand alone, the camera cliché will drown you among clones: find the difference in the name (Zoom), a character (Discord) or the shapes. And test the mark in a phone's call list — 40 pixels next to twenty competitors is what real logo sizes look like in the wild.
