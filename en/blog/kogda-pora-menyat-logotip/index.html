When It's Time to Change Your Logo — 7 Honest Signs

"Maybe it's time to refresh the logo?" — the question resurfaces in every company, usually right after a new marketing lead arrives. The trouble is that changing a mark is both harmful and useful — depending on when. Sber used a logo change to announce its transformation from a bank into an ecosystem, while hundreds of companies spent millions on redesigns nobody noticed. Here are the honest signs a mark actually needs changing — and the cases when you should leave it alone.

TL;DR

It's time to change the logo when it technically fails (falls apart at small sizes, no vector source), lies about the business (the company changed, the mark didn't) or is legally vulnerable. It's not time when the team is merely "tired of it": audiences take years to bond with a mark, and that capital is worth more than freshness. An update ≠ a replacement: most cases call for evolution, like Google or Chrome.

Terms first: redesign is a spectrum

Between "leave as is" and "draw from scratch" lies a whole scale:

DegreeWhat changesExample
Cleanupproportions, details, colorChrome: 4 iterations of one wheel
Modernizationtypeface, shapes, era styleGoogle 2015: serifs → sans
New marknew symbol, same brandSber 2020: circle → gradient chevron
Full rebrandname, mark, positioningTwitter → X

The further right, the more expensive and risky — past this point you're no longer redesigning, you're rebranding. Most problems are solved by the first two steps — good news, because evolution preserves accumulated recognition.

##7 signs it's time

1. The mark falls apart on modern surfaces. The logo was drawn for a business card and a signboard, but now it must live in a favicon, an avatar and an app icon. If it turns to mush at 32 pixels, that's not a matter of taste — it's a technical defect. This exact defect triggered the mass logo simplification of the 2010s.

2. No proper source files. The company is 15 years old and the logo exists as a JPG on a white background that everyone stretches as best they can. A missing vector source is a redesign trigger by itself: redrawing is inevitable anyway, so better do it deliberately.

3. The business changed, the mark didn't. The classic case is Sber: the bank became an ecosystem of dozens of services, and the old mark with the word "bank" physically couldn't cover the new products, as the Sber logo history lays out in detail. A mark that describes a company that no longer exists works against you.

4. The mark drags a bad reputation behind it. Rare but decisive: Microsoft deliberately killed Internet Explorer's recognizable "e" because it had come to mean "slow old browser" — the new Edge got a wave instead of a letter, one of several such moves among browser logos. When brand memory hurts, a break beats continuity.

5. You're indistinguishable from competitors. If your mark gets confused with the neighbors' on a shelf or in search results, it fails its primary job. Especially common in categories with settled clichés: blue shields for banks, green leaves for eco brands — exactly the mechanism behind color psychology in logos.

6. Legal vulnerability. The mark isn't registered, or resembles someone's registered one, or the rights still sit with a freelance contractor. Each is a time bomb: better to register a logo on your own schedule than to find out too late whether you could even use someone else's mark after a legal claim lands.

7. The mark can't be reproduced. Five gradients, shadows and 12 colors — and you need one-color print, engraving and embroidery. If half your surfaces require inventing a simplified version on the fly, there is no system — and that's a sign: every mark must have a working black-and-white version.

##3 cases when you should NOT change it

Bad reasons for a redesign

"We're tired of it" The team sees the logo daily and burns out on it in a year; the audience sees it for seconds and bonds over years. Team fatigue is the worst advisor.

"New CMO" A redesign as a show of activity spends recognition capital on an internal KPI. The mark belongs to the audience, not the org chart.

"Everyone's updating, so should we" Logo trends pass; the mark stays. Chasing a trend only makes sense if signs 1-7 above also apply.

The counter‑example is Safari: the compass has remained essentially unchanged since 2003 — because the metaphor works, the mark scales, and none of the seven signs apply. Not changing is a strategy too, and often the best one.

Decided to change: minimizing the risk

  1. Write down what exactly is broken. Walk through the seven signs and record concrete defects — that is your brief, and it's what you bring when you go commission a logo from a designer. "Want something fresher" is not a brief.
  2. Pick the degree. Cleanup, modernization or a new mark — see the table above. Default to the minimum sufficient step: recognition capital beats freshness.
  3. Keep the anchors. A color, a silhouette or a signature detail must survive the redesign — Yandex changed its typeface and mark but stayed red‑and‑black, as the Yandex logo history shows.
  4. Test technically before launch. The mark at 16 px, in one color, on dark, in SVG, in motion — yes, all the way to an animated logo — every check is cheaper before release than after.
  5. Prepare the explanation. Audiences read a mark change as a loss unless you tell them the logic — remember the Firefox "they removed the fox" meme. A "why we did it" post is a mandatory part of the launch.
  6. Update everything at once. The old mark on the signboard plus the new one on the website reads as chaos. Map the surfaces in advance against logo sizes for every platform, then register the new version.
A quick self-test

Count how many of the 7 signs apply to your mark. 0-1 — don't touch it; 2-3 — plan an evolution (cleanup or modernization); 4+ — put a full redesign into next year's budget, and check what it typically costs first.

Bottom line

Logos get changed not because they are old, but because they stopped working — technically, semantically or legally. While the mark does its job, its capital outweighs any freshness; once it doesn't, delaying costs more than changing. Successful evolutions and revolutions are in the catalog and in our breakdowns: Sber, Yandex, VK, Instagram.
