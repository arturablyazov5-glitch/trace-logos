The History of the Smiley — From the 1963 Yellow Badge to Emoji

The yellow smiley face is the most reproduced drawing in human history: older than the internet, it outlived every fashion and was finally reborn as the emoji we send by the billions daily. It also has specific authors, exact birth dates — and a detective story of how a $45 drawing became a brand worth hundreds of millions while earning its creator almost nothing. Here's the full biography: from an insurance‑company badge to Unicode.

TL;DR

1963 — artist Harvey Ball spends 10 minutes and earns $45 drawing a yellow face for an insurance company; 1970s — the Spain brothers add "Have a Happy Day" and sell millions of items, while France's Franklin Loufrani registers "Smiley" as a trademark and builds a licensing empire; 1982 — Scott Fahlman proposes the text smiley :-); 1999 — Shigetaka Kurita draws the first emoji. The whole line — from the face to the emoji catalog — took 36 years.

1963Ten minutes and forty‑five dollars

State Mutual Life Assurance of Massachusetts had been through a rough merger and needed to lift employee morale. Artist Harvey Ball was asked to draw something encouraging for the internal campaign. By his own account he spent ten minutes: a yellow circle, two oval eyes, a smile. The fee was $45; neither he nor the company registered any rights — what happens when rights go unregistered, this story illustrates better than any textbook.

Ball's design is easy to tell from imitations: the eyes are narrow ovals at different angles, the smile is not a perfect arc but a slightly crooked line with "dimples" at the ends. Living imperfection — the same reason the Starbucks siren got her asymmetry: perfect geometry reads as dead.

The 1970s: commercialization without the author

What followed would today be called going viral. In 1970 Bernard and Murray Spain of Philadelphia added the slogan "Have a Happy Day" and started stamping badges, mugs and stickers — tens of millions sold within a couple of years. Meanwhile in France, journalist Franklin Loufrani began printing the face in a newspaper as a good‑news marker — and, unlike everyone else, registered the "Smiley" mark (1972). His family's Smiley Company earns hundreds of millions in licensing to this day — the benchmark illustration that rights decide more than authorship.

Harvey Ball watched it all philosophically. In 1999 he founded World Smile Day (the first Friday of October) — "so that at least one day a year the smile works for good deeds, not commerce"

1982Colon, hyphen, bracket

The story's second branch grew in computer networks. On September 19, 1982, Carnegie Mellon professor Scott Fahlman proposed on the university bulletin board that jokes be marked with the sequence :-) and serious posts with :-(. The problem was real: flat text hides irony, and jokes kept detonating into arguments — we explain why text needs emotional markup separately.

Text smileys spread instantly and spawned an entire language — from the Western "sideways" tradition of ;-) to Japan's upright kaomoji (^_^), covered in a separate article. Fahlman's post, incidentally, was archaeologically recovered from a backup tape in 2002 — now a museum piece of the digital age.

1999The face becomes an emoji

Act three — Japan. Shigetaka Kurita, a designer at carrier NTT DoCoMo, drew a set of 176 pictograms at 12×12 pixels for mobile internet — faces among them. How emoji traveled from Japanese phones into Unicode and onto every keyboard is told in what emoji are and where they came from; what matters here is the lineage: the yellow circle with eyes and a smile 😊 is Ball's face's direct descendant, down to the canonical yellow.

Today "smileys" are the largest emoji category: the Smileys & Emotion section of our catalog holds a hundred and a half yellow faces for every shade of feeling — each drawn differently by every vendor.

What the smiley teaches about branding

The face's history concentrates the lessons we unpack across our logo articles:

  1. Simplicity beats everything. Three elements — circle, eyes, smile — survived six decades without a redesign. No trend managed to age it.
  2. Rights matter more than authorship. Ball got $45, Loufrani an empire: registration decided everything.
  3. A symbol is stronger than a word. The face works in every culture without translation — the property every global brand fights for (the Starbucks siren plays in the same league).
  4. Living imperfection. Ball's crooked smile is more human than a perfect arc — a device designers now build in deliberately.
