The Bento Grid — The Modular Layout That Took Over 2026 Design

A grid of neat cell‑tiles of different sizes, each with its own meaning, flooded websites, presentations, and landing pages in 2026. It's called the bento grid, by analogy with the Japanese bento box, where each dish sits in its own section. Let's break down what a bento grid is, where it came from, why it became a standard, and how to use it well — including for presenting logos and identity.

TL;DR

A bento grid is a modular layout of rectangular cells of different sizes, assembled into a single composition, like in a Japanese bento box. Each cell is a complete block (text, image, number, logo). The style became a 2026 standard: it structures a lot of information, adapts flexibly to the screen, and looks good. The key is keeping a unified grid and hierarchy, or the composition falls apart.

What a bento grid is

A bento grid is a layout method where content is split into rectangular cells of different sizes, densely assembled into an overall composition with no "holes" The name came from the Japanese bento food box, where each dish sits neatly in its own section.

The key idea: each cell is an independent, complete block. One large tile can carry the main point, several small ones — details, numbers, icons. Together they read as a unified but structured showcase.

Where the trend came from

Modular grids have existed in design for a long time, but "bento" in its current form was popularized by the presentations of major tech brands — above all Apple at its product events. From there the style spread to landing pages, dashboards, and portfolios. By 2026 the bento grid became one of the most recognizable layout techniques.

Why bento became a 2026 standard

  • It structures a lot of information. Cells of different sizes let you show both primary and secondary content on one screen without turning it into a wall of text.
  • It adapts flexibly. Tiles rearrange easily for the screen width — this naturally fits the adaptive approach.
  • It looks neat. A unified grid creates a sense of order and deliberation even with a lot of content.
  • It's versatile. It works on a website, in a presentation, and on social.

Where to use a bento grid

  • Landing and home pages — a showcase of product features in tiles.
  • Dashboards — numbers and metrics by cell.
  • Presentations — an overview slide instead of a bullet list.
  • Portfolios and collections — including galleries of logos and identity.
Bento and presenting logos

A bento grid is great for showing a set of logos or versions of one mark: each cell is a separate logo or its application (on a light background, dark, in a favicon). For crisp tiles use SVG — it doesn't "blur" in any cell, unlike raster.

How to make a bento grid correctly

  1. Keep a unified grid. All cells must obey a common modular grid with equal spacing. Mismatched spacing destroys the effect.
  2. Set a hierarchy. One or two large cells — the main point, the rest — support. If all tiles are equal, the eye gets lost.
  3. One meaning per cell. Don't cram everything into a tile; each carries one thought.
  4. A unified cell style. The same rounding, spacing, colors — part of a design system.
  5. Check on mobile. Tiles must rearrange nicely into a narrow column, not break.
Bento ≠ just tiles

The mistake is cutting content into random rectangles and calling it "bento" Without a unified grid, hierarchy, and grouping logic, you get visual noise — a typical design mistake. Bento's power is precisely in order: each cell is in its place and of its size for a reason.

Bento among 2026 trends

The bento grid is the "structural" trend of the year, and it already has an antipode: brutalism with a deliberately broken, anti‑grid layout. About it — in brutalism in web design. And the overall balance of order and brand expressiveness is held by a design system and consistency.

The short takeaway

A bento grid is a modular layout of cells of different sizes assembled into a single composition, where each tile carries one complete meaning. The style came from big brands' presentations and became a 2026 standard because it structures a lot of information, adapts flexibly, and looks neat. The key rule is a unified grid, hierarchy, and grouping logic: without them "bento" turns into a set of random rectangles. For crisp presentation of logos in cells, use SVG.
