A Logo Brief — How to Set the Task So You Don't Redo It Ten Times

"Draw something modern, minimalist, but with wow factor, and something everyone likes" If a logo commission sounds roughly like this, you can prepare in advance for ten rounds of revisions, frayed nerves, and a final "not it, redo everything" Neither the designer nor the client alone is to blame here — the blame is a brief that essentially didn't exist.

A brief is a boring‑looking technical task that actually decides a logo's fate before the first sketch. Let's break down why a vague statement guarantees failure and how to set the task so you get a strong mark without endless redos.

Why "make it pretty" doesn't work

"Pretty" isn't a task but a matter of taste. The client has one picture in their head, the designer another, and until they match, any sketch will be "not it" The problem is there's no criterion: there's nothing to judge whether the designer hit the mark. Hence revisions in circles — each new version just guesses at someone else's taste.

A good brief removes the guessing. It turns a vague "I want it pretty" into checkable requirements: who the mark is for, what it should convey, where it will live, what definitely shouldn't be there. And then a sketch gets a measure — not "like it / don't like it" but "solves the task / doesn't"

What a brief should actually describe

A strong brief answers not "how to look" but "what work the mark should do" Here's its semantic core.

About the business. What the company does, how it differs from competitors, what its values are. A logo must support the brand's essence — which means that essence must be named. What makes a mark strong at all — in what a logo is.

About the audience. Who the brand is for. A mark for teenagers and a mark for notaries are built differently, and it's the audience, not tastes, that decides. The color, shape, and voice depend on it too.

About the feeling. How the brand should seem: reliable, bold, warm, technical? Three or four adjectives give the designer direction more precisely than any "make it stylish"

About application. Where the logo will live: website, sign, app, packaging, marketplace? This determines what the mark must be — for example, adaptive for small sizes.

About constraints. What's definitely not allowed: which colors, associations, images are unacceptable. Sometimes "what not to do" is more useful than "what to do"

References are a language, not a template to copy

Gather 3-5 marks you like and — crucially — explain why: "I like the simplicity," "I like the warm color," "I like that it reads on a button" A reference without an explanation the designer will read their own way. Even more useful is showing anti‑references — what you dislike and why. Just don't turn a reference into a "make it the same" task: that's the path to someone else's logo, not your own.

A list of brief questions

To avoid building a brief from scratch, go through these questions — the answers are the ready task:

  1. What does the company do and what's its main difference?
  2. Who is your audience (age, style, expectations)?
  3. In which 3-4 words should the brand be perceived?
  4. Who are the competitors and how do you want to differ from them?
  5. Where will the logo be used (list all places)?
  6. Do you need just a mark, just a name, or a combination?
  7. Are there mandatory elements or colors (e.g., a brand color)?
  8. What definitely shouldn't be there?
  9. Examples of marks you like and dislike — with an explanation of why.
  10. Deadlines, budget, output file formats.

This same list will help when you commission a logo from a designer — it immediately sets a common language. And if you make the mark yourself, a brief is still needed: it keeps you from getting lost in your own "let's also try this"

A brief saves money, it doesn't waste time

Filling out a brief seems like extra bureaucracy when "it's all clear anyway" But "clear anyway" is exactly what breeds redos: each revision costs time and money. Half an hour on a brief at the start saves weeks of revisions at the end. This is especially noticeable with a logo's cost: a clear brief is the cheapest way to get the right result the first time.

The main thing

A logo brief isn't bureaucracy but a way to remove guessing: it turns a taste‑based "make it pretty" into a checkable task and thereby saves weeks of revisions. A good brief describes not "how to look" but what work the mark should do: the business's essence, the audience, the needed feeling, places of use, and constraints. References are useful, but only with an explanation of why you like them — and not as a template to copy. Go through the ten questions before the first sketch — and it doesn't matter whether you commission the mark or make it yourself: a clear task almost always yields a strong logo faster and cheaper.
