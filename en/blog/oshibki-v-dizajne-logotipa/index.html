10 Logo Design Mistakes — Check Your Own Mark

A bad logo is rarely "ugly" — more often it just doesn't work: it crumbles in an avatar, vanishes on dark backgrounds, costs extra to print and can't be registered. Here are the ten most common mistakes — compositional to legal. Run your mark down the list: each error takes a minute to catch and is far cheaper to fix before launch than after.

TL;DR

The top 10: detail clutter; illegibility at 16 px; color dependence; trend‑chasing over longevity; an unlicensed font; clipart and templates; missing versions; a raster‑only source; niche look‑alikes; a skipped trademark check. Below — how to catch and cure each.

##1. Detail clutter

Gradient + shadow + five elements + two fonts — a mark that "shows everything" is remembered for nothing. Every great logo fits one phrase: Telegram — the paper plane, Apple — the apple. Test: describe your mark in one sentence over the phone. Can't? Simplify — today almost every major brand does.

##2. Small‑size illegibility

The mark was approved on a presentation screen, but it will live in a 16×16 favicon and a 40 px avatar. Thin lines fuse, small text vanishes. Test: shrink the mark to fingernail size — the silhouette must stay recognizable. Big brands keep a separate, simplified app icon tuned specifically for small sizes.

##3. Color dependence

If the mark is distinguishable only in brand colors, it breaks on engraving, receipts, faxes and single‑color print — logo-for-print has its own demands, and color doesn't always survive there. Test: convert the logo to black and white. The form must work alone; color is an amplifier, not a crutch, however well it's chosen by color psychology.

##4. Trend‑chasing

The "AI‑service" gradient glow — an effect we dissected in the neural-network logos roundup — or the season's fashionable font dates the mark to its birth year. A logo lives 10-20 years; a trend lasts 2-3. Rule: the mark's core stays timeless; the trendy bits live only in the replaceable layer (gradient, animation).

##5. An unlicensed font

The mark is set in a pretty font downloaded "somewhere" — and the font's license says "personal use only". For a commercial brand that's a lawsuit waiting. Test: name your logo's font and its license. Don't know? Find out before launch by learning how logo fonts are actually chosen. The robust fix: outline the text and hold a confirmed license for the source font.

##6. Clipart and templates

The stock "idea" lightbulb, a Canva template, an untouched AI generation with its typical artifacts — covered in detail here — hundreds of other companies wear the same mark. It doesn't differentiate you and won't register as a trademark. Test: reverse‑image‑search your own logo. Found twins? It's not your mark.

##7. Missing versions

One "logo_final.jpg" for every occasion is not a kit. You need: the full version, the compact mark, monochrome, and an inverse for dark backgrounds — that's the full set of logo types. Test: place the mark on a dark background and in a round avatar. If anything needs "fixing on the spot", versions are missing.

##8. A raster‑only source

The logo exists only as PNG/JPG: on signage it turns to mush, and every scaling pass degrades it further. The master has to be vector — here's why that difference matters so much; a raster mark needs tracing into vector once, then stored as the master in one of the vector formats.

##9. Looking like the niche neighbors

The blue shield of an insurer, the green cross of a pharmacy, the messenger bubble — category clichés make a brand invisible, and it shows clearly across bank logos, messenger logos and marketplace logos. Test: line your mark up with five competitors (find them in the catalog) and ask an outsider to spot yours in three seconds.

##10. The skipped legal check

A mark can look unique and still infringe an existing trademark — risking a forced rebrand at the worst possible moment. Before launch: search the trademark databases, then register the mark; when commissioning a designer, get a written transfer of exclusive rights — details in how to commission a logo from a designer. What's allowed and not with others' marks is covered separately.

Found some mistakes?

Not everything demands a redesign. Mistakes 5, 7, 8 and 10 are "infrastructure": license, versions, vector and registration bolt onto the existing mark without changing its form. Mistakes 1-4, 6 and 9 concern the mark itself: the cure is careful evolution instead of a full rebrand — simplify and clean the form while keeping the recognizable core, the way Google and Instagram regularly do.

Visual literacy is the best prevention: browse the logo catalog and notice how big brands' marks pass every item on this list. Building from scratch? Start with how to make a logo yourself and what types of logos even exist.
