How to Write a Portfolio Case Study That Brings In Work

The typical case study opens like this: "A company approached me. We ran a briefing, studied the competition, assembled a moodboard, and drew more than forty explorations of the mark." Then come the construction grids, the clear space, and mockups on a mug and a building façade.

It is written in the order the work happened. It gets read in a different order: the visitor scrolls to the pictures, looks at them for ten seconds, and closes the tab. The text stays unread, and with it disappears the only thing separating you from a hundred other portfolios with similar pictures.

What separates you is the way you make decisions. A picture can be copied; somebody else's logic is far harder to repeat, and that logic is what a client buys. So a case study gets assembled in reverse: first what changed, then how you got there.

Who opens your portfolio, and with what question

A case study has exactly two kinds of reader, and both want an answer to the same question.

The first is a prospective client. They arrived with a problem resembling one you already solved and are checking whether theirs can be trusted to you. What interests them is what changed for your previous client and what it cost in time. The forty explorations mean nothing to them, because they are buying an outcome.

The second is an art director or a hiring manager. They are looking at whether you can explain decisions, and here the process does matter, in a narrow form — a chain of choices with stated reasons. A work report doesn't fill that role. A rejected direction with an explanation of why it was rejected says more about you than the final picture.

Both questions reduce to one: how do you think. So the case study has to show thinking. Whether the reader ever reaches the place where it is shown is decided by the first screen.

Why a case study starts with the outcome

Before the first screen the reader sees the case's title in the list of works and decides from it whether to open anything at all. "Logo for a coffee chain" reports a category, and there are twenty such lines in the list. "A mark for a coffee chain, legible from across the street" names the problem and already differs from its neighbours. The title runs on the same rule as the whole case: what the work was done for goes in the first few words.

The first screen should state what changed for the client. "One mark now covers the sign, the app, and the paperwork; before, every carrier got its own version." A sentence like that speaks the language of the client's problems, which is why it gets read.

Compare it with the usual opening: "We ran a briefing and studied the competition." Those words describe your labour, and in the first second the reader wants their own result. They will pay for the labour later and more willingly, once they see what they are paying for.

The resulting order inside the case is the client's problem, the outcome, then the path to it. Frame the problem through the constraint you had to beat — that alone makes it interesting. "A twelve‑café chain needed a mark legible from across the street and on a cup" works better than "the client needed a new logo." The constraint explains why the solution looks the way it does and removes half of the reader's future questions.

The same principle runs at sentence level: strong words go in the first two or three, because the end of a phrase doesn't always get read. "Cut the path to an order from seven steps to three" instead of "As a result of the work carried out, it was possible to reduce…"

Adjectives that prove nothing, and what replaces them

After the outcome comes the description of the solution, and this is where case studies break in the same way every time. In come the words "clean," "modern," "reflecting the brand's values." They describe the author's attitude to their own work and report nothing about the mark. The same failure gets a full treatment in the piece on why strong logos fail on words.

Every such adjective can be swapped for a claim the reader verifies against the pictures in that same case study.

AdjectiveVerifiable claim
"Clean"The mark has four anchor points and draws in a single stroke
"Universal"Works in one colour — for engraving and stamps
"Scales well"Legible at a 16-pixel favicon
"Flexible"Three shape variants for three size ranges — an adaptive system
"Unique"No mark with a similar shape in the registry for the relevant class

Numbers bring their own difficulty. "Conversion grew by 40%" with no baseline and no period reads as embellishment, and sometimes is one. A number works when a reference point stands beside it: "three months after launch, enquiries run at 120 a month against 85 before." With no access to the client's metrics, describing a verifiable property of the work itself is more honest than claiming somebody else's sales growth.

A separate case is work under NDA, or a project that never shipped. Publishing it is fine once the identifying parts are gone: replace the client's name with a description ("a coffee chain in a city of a million"), leave revenue figures out entirely. The value survives intact, because what people buy from you is the logic of the decisions, and that isn't tied to the client's name. A project halted midway works too, provided you state honestly at which stage it stopped and why.

Team work resolves the same way. A case saying "we developed" when an eight‑person studio did the work leaves a hiring manager with one question: which part was yours. The answer takes one line and removes the suspicion entirely: "on this project I owned the mark and the icon system; another designer handled typography." That honesty works in your favour, because it shows you understand the boundaries of your responsibility. A case built on a vague "we" gets tested with questions at the interview, where the same thing comes out, only no longer to your advantage.

Why the headings inside a case study matter more than the text

Case studies get skimmed, so the headings work for the whole text. A person slides down the page, catches two or three subheadings, and decides from them whether to read the paragraphs beneath. A heading reading "The task" contributes nothing at that moment.

What works is a heading that answers a question or removes an objection. "Why we dropped the illustrative mark" instead of "Concept." "How the mark behaves at 16 pixels" instead of "Scaling." "How long it took and what made up the timeline" instead of "Process." A heading like that can be read apart from the text and still means something.

The check is simple: write out all the subheadings in order and read the resulting list. If it tells you what work was done and how it ended, the case study works even for someone who read no paragraph at all. If you got "Task," "Solution," "Result," the case rests on its pictures, and everyone's pictures look alike.

What to do with the process when it does matter

The process doesn't have to go; it has to move and shrink. Placed after the outcome, it stops being a report and becomes evidence: you promised the solution wasn't accidental, and now you show it.

Forty explorations are unnecessary; three rejected ones are, each with a stated reason for the rejection. "This one read on the sign and fell apart in the app." "This one repeated a competitor's shape from a neighbouring city." "This one needed two colours, and part of the print run goes out in one." Three such paragraphs show that decisions ran on criteria. Forty pictures in a row show only stamina.

The same applies to talk about deadlines and money. A promise that sounds too good needs its mechanics explained: "the budget didn't grow, because every stage was costed before the start and fixed in the contract." A client reading your case before commissioning a logo is looking for exactly that predictability. It also helps to show what you were handed: a case with a visible original brief is more convincing than one where the task appeared from nowhere.

What to show instead of a mug mockup

Mockups sell a picture while a case sells a decision, so pick carriers by the problem. A café mark — a photograph of the sign in the rain and a cup at arm's length. A bank mark — the icon on a home screen among thirty others. A nonprofit mark — one‑colour printing on a letterhead. Each of those frames proves a specific claim from the text, and a mug proves nothing.

What a case study should not contain

Last comes the short list of what gets cut during the final read.

Excuses. "The deadline slipped because approvals dragged on." "The client picked the weaker option." The reader came for confidence, and phrases like these take it away. If the choice wasn't yours, describing the chosen option without commentary is the honest move.

New doubts. "Further iterations may be required" with no explanation adds anxiety where there was none. Every sentence in a case either removes a question or creates one, and the second kind has no place there.

Anything that doesn't affect the reader's decision. The client company's founding story, the list of software you used, the acknowledgements. Those paragraphs delete cleanly, and their absence shortens the path to the point.

And the phrasings that expose a generator: "this is not just a logo but a brand promise," "in today's world it is important to understand." How to find and replace them is covered separately.

What to check before publishing

A case study brings in work when it shows how decisions get made. Everything else follows: the outcome sits on the first screen, the process comes after it and in shortened form, adjectives give way to verifiable claims, and the headings read apart from the text.

The check takes five minutes. Read the first screen and ask what changed for the client. Write out the subheadings and see whether they add up to a story. Count the adjectives like "clean" — the target is zero. Confirm that every rejected direction carries a stated reason.

What remains is making sure the case reads as connected text: why readers quit halfway.
