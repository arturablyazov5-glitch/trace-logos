Skeuomorphism and "Liquid Glass" — Why Realism Returned in 2026

Remember icons that looked like real objects? A notepad with stitched leather, a voice recorder with a metal mesh, a "save" button as a floppy disk with a glare. Ten years ago flat design declared all this bad taste and wiped it to zero — clean silhouettes without shadows and textures remained. It seemed realism in interfaces had died forever. And in 2026 it returned, and to understand why, it's worth first figuring out what it was banished for.

What flat design killed realism for

Skeuomorphism is when a digital element pretends to be a real thing: a button looks like a real button you want to press, a switch — like a physical toggle, a surface — like glass, metal, or paper. The idea was sound: a person intuitively understands how to handle something that resembles a familiar object, and the interface leaned on that everyday experience.

But imitation turned out to have a side effect. All that leather, metal, and shadow took up space, added weight, and distracted — and on the small smartphone screens that were conquering the world right then, the detail hurt readability. Flat design won precisely because it solved this: clean form and color read faster and scaled more easily. By the same logic and in the very same years, brands massively simplified their logos — stripping the excess for clarity on small media. Realism lost not because it was ugly but because it was impractical for the new environment.

Why the pendulum swung back

If it were only about practicality, realism wouldn't have returned — flat design hasn't gone anywhere and is still convenient. But over ten years fatigue accumulated: when all interfaces became equally flat, they became equally faceless. People wanted depth and "tangibility" — the same feeling that brought 3D logos and soft gradients back into fashion.

Fatigue alone, though, wouldn't have been enough. Realism left partly because it cost weak devices dearly — and here everything changed: screens and processors caught up, and generative AI learned to reproduce real materials with such precision that rich texture stopped being a luxury. The technological barrier that once helped flat design win simply disappeared.

And then came a concrete push — "liquid glass" It's a direct evolution of glassmorphism: if "frosted glass" used to be a static picture, now it became a behavior. The surface reacts to what's beneath it, refracts light, "flows" after the content. And precisely because it's alive rather than drawn, it hits another 2026 demand — for micro-interactions that feel physical: a button softly springs, a switch clicks. Realism returned not for one reason but because several forces converged at one point.

The starting point was Apple, and then a chain reaction

"Liquid glass" has a precise birthdate: June 2025, the WWDC keynote, where Apple unveiled Liquid Glass — a new visual language for iOS 26, iPadOS, macOS, watchOS, and tvOS. Buttons, navigation bars, icons, and widgets became translucent "glass" surfaces: they refract whatever's beneath them, "flow" as you scroll, and react to a finger's motion instead of just being drawn with a static glare, the way iOS 6's skeuomorphism was a decade and a half earlier. Starting September 2026, with Xcode 27, developers will lose the option to turn the new style off via a compatibility flag — Liquid Glass becomes mandatory by default. But the market reacted even faster than that deadline.

First in the chain was Telegram: in early 2026 "liquid glass" arrived on its iOS app — translucent layers and refraction in screen transitions, with a dedicated toggle in settings for weaker devices — and by February it reached Android too, where the interface was rebuilt from scratch around the same language and the classic side menu was swapped for a bottom navigation bar, unifying the look with iOS. Right behind it, Instagram started rolling out its own glass bottom bar, and the same conversation is happening around WhatsApp. It's the same mechanic that's played out before with logo simplification: one major player sets a new visual standard, and the market follows, because users get used to the new language fast and start seeing the old interface as dated.

There was also pushback — the same kind that once buried the first skeuomorphism. Some users complained that text on "glass" panels loses contrast over busy backgrounds, and Telegram's Android redesign got a mixed reception. That's exactly the trade‑off from the next section: "liquid glass" is striking, but it demands the same accessibility discipline as any other return to realism.

But it's no longer that "leather" realism

Here it's important not to be fooled. Realism's return isn't a rollback to the stitched leather and wooden shelves of old interfaces; that style went away deservedly, for overload, and a literal repeat would run into the same wall again. The new skeuomorphism absorbed flat design's lesson and therefore behaves more restrained: a subtle shadow, a delicate texture, realistic light — as an accent on one or two elements, not a carpet of "real" textures across the whole screen.

Essentially it's a compromise of two eras: from realism they took depth and tactility, from flat design they kept cleanliness and readability. And this compromise holds exactly as long as you remember why flat design won in the first place.

Where realism becomes a problem again

Forget about moderation and back come exactly the troubles realism was banished for. An abundance of textures, shadows, and glares quickly turns an interface into mush and hurts accessibility: readability beats flashiness. And realistic materials are heavier than flat graphics on top of that, and that's a hit to speed — the very topic of sustainable web design, where every extra kilobyte costs energy. Realism works in moderation; as the basis of everything it self‑destructs.

What this means for your logo

From all this follows a practical conclusion for the mark. Realistic texture and volume really can add "premiumness" to a logo — but the very properties that make them striking in a "hero" scene make them unfit for a working one. A heavy realistic mark won't survive on a favicon, will fall apart in single-color print, and will "blur" at small size — it needs, like a 3D logo, a simplified flat version, that same adaptive system where each size has its own form. And since realistic effects usually live in heavy raster, keep the base version in SVG anyway.

So the line is simple: realism is fitting where the mark has room to unfold — in a splash screen, on a "hero" screen, in a presentation. And a working signature logo still requires a clean flat form you won't mind shrinking to 16 pixels. One doesn't cancel the other: a strong brand has both and knows where to show which.

In short

Realism lost to flat design not for its beauty but for its impracticality on small screens — and for the same reason brands then simplified logos. It returned in 2026 because several forces converged at once: fatigue with the equally flat, materials made cheap by technology and AI, and "liquid glass" — a living, light‑reactive evolution of glassmorphism that hit the general demand for tactility. But the new skeuomorphism is more restrained than the old: it takes depth from realism and cleanliness from flat, and collapses the moment you forget the moderation that guards accessibility and weight. For a logo the direct consequence is: realism for "hero" scenes, while the working version needs a clean flat form in vector with a simplified adaptive variant.
